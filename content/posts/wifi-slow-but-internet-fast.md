Your internet plan's speed applies at the modem. Wi-Fi is a separate wireless link between your router and each device, and that link has its own limits: distance, walls, the band it uses, interference, and what the device itself can do. That's why a speed test on a laptop plugged into the router can look great while your phone crawls in the bedroom.

The good news is that this split also tells you where to look. Your provider is off the hook; the problem is somewhere between the router and the device.

## How do I confirm it's Wi-Fi and not my internet?

Plug a laptop into the router with an Ethernet cable and run a speed test. The FCC says a direct Ethernet connection gives the highest speeds. Verizon's speed-test guidance suggests disconnecting any VPN and pausing streaming apps so the result is clean.

If the wired result is close to your plan, your internet is fine. Then run the same test over Wi-Fi next to the router, and again in the slow room. The size of the gap is your clue.

Say you pay for 500 Mbps. A wired laptop gets 480. Your phone gets 60 in the bedroom two walls away. The internet is fine; the phone's wireless link is the weak point.

## What actually slows Wi-Fi down?

### Distance and walls

Signal weakens with distance and with each wall or floor it crosses. Brick, concrete, metal and large appliances are the worst. The FCC recommends putting the router in a central location. In Tom's Guide's September 2026 tests, mesh kits that managed 1.4 to 1.9 Gbps at 15 feet fell to between 20.8 and 150.1 Mbps at 90 feet in a brick, multi-story home.

### The wrong band

The FCC says 2.4 GHz is also used by many household devices, so it can be crowded. The 5 GHz band offers faster speeds and may be less crowded by neighbors' networks, though it doesn't carry as far. A phone sitting on 2.4 GHz in a room with a clear 5 GHz signal is a common cause of "slow Wi-Fi, fast internet."

### The device itself

Older phones, laptops and smart TVs have slower Wi-Fi radios. A device that supports only an older Wi-Fi standard can't use the speed a newer router offers, however fast your plan is. This is why one device can be slow while others are fine.

### Neighbors and interference

If your router's Wi-Fi settings show a long list of nearby networks, the FCC says their signals could affect your performance. In apartment buildings, that often shows up in the evening.

### Too many devices at once

The FCC notes that when many wireless devices share a Wi-Fi network, it can affect performance. A few video calls, a game download and several 4K streams can saturate the wireless side even when the internet connection has room.

### The router

An older router, one with outdated firmware, or one tucked in a cabinet can be the bottleneck. Apple recommends setting routers to install firmware updates automatically. If you use a combined modem-router from your provider and it's several years old, it may be a limiting factor too.

### Settings

Apple recommends using one network name across all bands, leaving the channel on automatic, and setting the radio mode to allow all Wi-Fi versions (it lists "All" as preferred) so each device can connect at the fastest mode it supports. Apple also suggests a 20 MHz channel width for 2.4 GHz. A router that was set to an older, restricted mode can hold back newer devices.

## How can I see what my device is actually connected to?

Look at the device's Wi-Fi details. Menu names vary by version, so treat these as starting points.

- **Windows:** open Settings, go to Network & internet, then Wi-Fi, and open your network's properties. It lists the network band, the Wi-Fi protocol and the link speed.
- **Mac:** hold the Option key and click the Wi-Fi icon in the menu bar. It shows the channel, the Wi-Fi mode and the transmit rate.
- **Phones:** iPhone and Android screens differ by model. If a phone isn't giving details, a free Wi-Fi analyzer app can show the band and signal strength.

Two things to look for. First, is the device on 2.4 GHz when 5 GHz is available? Second, is the Wi-Fi mode an older one? The link speed shown is the maximum the radio link is currently negotiating, and real download speeds are typically lower, so don't expect to see your full plan speed there.

## What should I do about it?

Work from the cheapest fix up:

1. **Move closer, or move the router.** If speed jumps near the router, it's coverage.
2. **Move the slow device to 5 GHz.** Forget the network and rejoin, or move closer so it chooses 5 GHz.
3. **Update the device and the router.** Both matter.
4. **Check what else is using the network** and pause big downloads.
5. **Wire what stays put.** TVs, consoles and desktops are better on Ethernet.
6. **Add coverage.** If the far room is simply too far, a mesh node placed halfway between the router and that room usually works better than one placed in the room itself. See our router-versus-mesh guide.
7. **Replace the router only after the above.** An old router that no longer gets updates, or one that's slow even beside it, is a fair candidate.

## What this means for you

If only one device is slow, fix that device first. If every device is slow in the same room, it's coverage. If everything is slow, even next to the router, look at the router, its settings and your neighbors' networks.

A faster internet plan won't help a Wi-Fi problem. Fix the wireless link before you pay for more speed.

## Bottom line

Fast internet and slow Wi-Fi aren't a contradiction. They're two different links. A cable test splits them apart, and the device's own Wi-Fi details show what's really happening on the wireless side.
