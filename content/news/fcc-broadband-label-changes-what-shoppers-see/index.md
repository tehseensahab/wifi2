On July 22, 2026, the Federal Communications Commission (FCC) adopted rules that change how internet providers present their broadband "nutrition labels", the standard price-and-speed summary providers must offer for each internet plan. Providers will be able to show a link or icon instead of the full label, describe it aloud on sales calls, and show some fees as a total. But those label changes are not in effect yet: a Federal Register notice published August 13, 2026 says they are delayed indefinitely.

This article is based on the FCC's order, its press release and the Federal Register notice. We have not tested any provider's labels, and the FCC's consumer labels page was not accessible to us when we researched this report, so we make no claims about what any specific provider currently displays.

## What is a broadband label, and what is still required?

A broadband label is a standardized summary of an internet plan's price, speeds and terms, shown when you shop. The FCC's July 2026 press release says providers must have a label for each standalone broadband plan they currently offer. The order says labels continue to show:

- Prices, including introductory rates, plus one-time and recurring fees.
- Data allowances.
- Contract term.
- Typical download and upload speeds, and latency (the delay before data starts to move).
- A link to the provider's network management practices.

The order also keeps two accessibility requirements: labels must stay easily accessible to people with disabilities at the point of sale, and must appear in English and in any other language the provider uses to market the service in the United States and its territories.

## What did the FCC change, and when does each change apply?

The Federal Register notice splits the order in two. This table keeps the two parts apart, because the headline changes depend on which part they sit in.

| Change | Rule section | Status per the August 13, 2026 Federal Register notice |
|---|---|---|
| Updated definition of "broadband internet access service" and new definition of "passthrough fee" | 47 CFR 8.1(b) | Effective September 14, 2026 |
| Link or icon allowed instead of the full label, at the point of sale and in account portals | 47 CFR 8.1(a)(2) | Delayed indefinitely |
| Phone sales: representatives may summarize the label orally | 47 CFR 8.1(a)(2) | Delayed indefinitely |
| Passthrough fees may be shown as a maximum or exact monthly total | 47 CFR 8.1(a)(1) | Delayed indefinitely |
| Machine-readable (spreadsheet-format) label file no longer required | 47 CFR 8.1(a)(3), removed | Delayed indefinitely |
| Two-year archive of labels for withdrawn plans no longer required | 47 CFR 8.1(a)(5), removed | Delayed indefinitely |

The order itself explains why: the changes to section 8.1(a) are not effective until the Office of Management and Budget (OMB) finishes any required Paperwork Reduction Act review, and the FCC's Consumer and Governmental Affairs Bureau will announce the date by public notice. The Federal Register notice says the Commission will publish a document announcing the effective date. It does not explain the delay, and we did not find an announcement of a date as of October 15, 2026.

In plain terms, as of that date the formal, announced change in force is the definitions. What providers must do on labels is, according to these documents, waiting on that later announcement.

## What will providers be allowed to do once the changes take effect?

These descriptions come from the order and apply only after the delayed provisions take effect.

**Link or icon at the point of sale.** Providers may show a link or icon instead of the label. The order says it must be "clearly identified as a way to access the label for the advertised plan," placed in close proximity to the plan, and connect directly to that plan's label. In account portals, providers must make a customer's current plan label easily accessible through a link or icon.

**Phone sales.** Representatives would not have to read the label word for word. The order lists the fields they must orally summarize: monthly price including fees, introductory rate and duration, typical download and upload speeds, latency, data allowance, contract term and early termination fees. It declined to adopt a mandatory script.

**Passthrough fees.** Providers may show these fees as a maximum or exact monthly total instead of itemizing each one. A maximum must be split into government and non-governmental amounts, with the fee types in each category described. The order says displayed amounts must not understate current fees and that the label must be revised if fees rise above the disclosed amount.

**Less paperwork for providers.** The separate spreadsheet-format file and the two-year archive of old labels would no longer be required, and references to the expired Affordable Connectivity Program are dropped.

The FCC says its aim is to make labels easier to use and reduce provider costs. We have not evaluated whether the changes achieve either aim. The order was approved by Chairman Carr and Commissioners Gomez and Trusty, each of whom issued a separate statement.

## What does this mean when you shop for internet?

For now, the practical advice does not change, and it applies whichever version of the label you see.

1. **Find the label for the exact plan.** Providers must have one for each standalone plan they currently offer. If you only see a link or icon, follow it to the plan you are buying.
2. **Look for the introductory rate and how long it lasts.** The order lists this as a core field. Compare the price after it ends, not just the first price. For example (hypothetical numbers): a plan at $50 a month for 12 months, then $70, costs $50 × 12 + $70 × 12 = $1,440 over 24 months, an average of $60 a month before taxes. Another plan at $62 a month with no promotion costs $1,488 over the same 24 months.
3. **Check fees.** Look at one-time and recurring fees, and whether any are shown as a single "up to" figure once that option is allowed. A maximum is a ceiling, not a guaranteed bill.
4. **Check data allowance and contract term.** These affect the real cost if you go over or leave early.
5. **Read speeds as "typical", not guaranteed.** Our guide to [how much internet speed you need]({{< relref "/guides/how-much-internet-speed-do-i-need" >}}) explains how to match a plan to your household, and our comparison of [fiber, cable and 5G home internet]({{< relref "/guides/fiber-vs-cable-vs-5g-home-internet" >}}) covers how the technologies differ.
6. **Check equipment charges.** Whether a router rental fee belongs on your bill is a separate question, covered in our guide to [buying your own router or using your provider's]({{< relref "/guides/buy-your-own-router-vs-isp-equipment" >}}).

## What happens next?

Two things to watch: the FCC's announcement of the effective date for the delayed section 8.1(a) changes, and, after that, whether providers actually switch to links, icons and totals. We will update this article when a date is announced.

## Bottom line

The FCC has decided to loosen how providers present broadband labels, but, according to the August 13, 2026 Federal Register notice, the label changes themselves are delayed indefinitely and only the definitions are in effect. Whatever form the label takes, the numbers worth comparing are the same: the price after the introductory period, total fees, the data allowance, the contract term and typical speeds.
