Yes, for now. The FCC's rule on foreign-produced routers generally bars changes to routers that were already approved for sale, but the FCC waived that bar for software and firmware updates that mitigate harm to US consumers. A notice released May 8, 2026 extended the waiver to "at least until January 1, 2029." The waiver does not cover hardware changes, and it does not require any manufacturer to keep issuing updates.

This is a news explainer based on FCC public notices. We read the FCC documents listed below, not manufacturer statements, and we have not tested any router's update process.

## What did the FCC do on March 23, 2026?

The FCC's Public Safety and Homeland Security Bureau released a public notice (DA 26-278) adding this entry to the Covered List: "Routers produced in a foreign country, except routers which have been granted a Conditional Approval by DoW or DHS." DoW is the Department of War and DHS is the Department of Homeland Security. The Covered List is the FCC's list of equipment that is barred from receiving new equipment authorizations, the FCC approval a radio device needs before it can be marketed in the US.

Three points from the notice and legal analysis of it:

- **New models are the target.** Covered equipment cannot get new equipment authorizations. The notice applies by place of production, not by company.
- **Conditional Approvals are exemptions.** DoW or DHS can grant them, for periods of up to 18 months. Routers that hold one are exempt from the list.
- **Existing authorizations remain valid.** The law firm Wiley's analysis says existing FCC authorizations stay valid unless the FCC acts to revoke or limit them. The notices we read do not themselves address whether previously authorized models can be sold or used, so we rely on that secondary analysis here. Our earlier [status report on conditional approvals]({{< relref "/news/fcc-router-conditional-approvals-status" >}}) covers which brands have been approved.

## Why would firmware updates be affected?

The FCC's rules treat changes to already-authorized equipment as "permissive changes," sorted into Class I and Class II. After the Covered List addition, revised rules at 47 CFR 2.932(b) and 2.1043(b) would bar permissive changes to covered equipment. Firmware that alters a router can count as such a change, so without a waiver even a security patch could have run into the rule. DA 26-286, also released March 23, 2026, waived that bar for software and firmware updates that mitigate harm to US consumers, on routers authorized before that date.

## What changed on May 8, 2026?

OET's notice DA 26-454 did three things, according to the FCC text:

| Change | Detail |
|---|---|
| Longer deadline | The waiver now runs "at least until January 1, 2029." The earlier router waiver ended March 1, 2027. |
| Wider coverage | It now covers Class II as well as Class I permissive changes, if they are software or firmware updates that mitigate harm to US consumers. |
| Conditions | It applies only to already-authorized devices. Grantees must still meet other FCC rules, including giving complete information and test results for Class II changes and not marketing modified equipment until the change is acknowledged as acceptable. |

The notice says updates covered include those that "patch vulnerabilities and facilitate compatibility with different operating systems." It also says OET will recommend that the full Commission consider codifying the waiver.

## What is still not allowed?

Hardware. A May 15, 2026 OET order, responding to a request from AT&T, states that hardware or other permissive changes to covered routers are not included in the waivers and remain prohibited. It granted AT&T a one-year waiver, to May 15, 2027, limited to its suppliers substituting substrate materials and memory modules in previously certified routers. That order says nothing that widens the firmware waiver, and it does not address whether previously authorized models can be sold.

## What does this mean for you?

Our reading, which is analysis and not an FCC statement:

- **The rule does not by itself cut off updates for a router you own,** as long as it was authorized before March 23, 2026 and the waiver stays in place.
- **The waiver is permission, not a promise.** Whether you receive patches depends on the manufacturer's own support policy. Check yours using our guide to [whether your router still gets security updates]({{< relref "/guides/is-my-router-still-getting-security-updates" >}}).
- **Dates matter if you are buying.** A new foreign-produced model needs a Conditional Approval to be authorized. For where individual brands stood, see our reporting on [TP-Link and the state lawsuits]({{< relref "/news/tp-link-state-lawsuits-what-owners-should-know" >}}) and our [large-house router guide]({{< relref "/guides/best-wifi-router-for-large-house" >}}).
- **Do not assume 2029 is guaranteed.** The FCC wrote "at least until," and the waiver can be revisited. We found no later notice as of October 14, 2026.

## Bottom line

For a router you already own, the FCC's rule does not stop firmware updates as long as its waiver stands, which is at least until January 1, 2029. What does decide your risk is whether your manufacturer keeps issuing patches, so check your model's support status now and turn on automatic updates.
