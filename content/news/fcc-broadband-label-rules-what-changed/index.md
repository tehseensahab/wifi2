The FCC's broadband label rules are changing, but not as fast as some headlines suggest. The Federal Communications Commission (FCC) voted on July 22, 2026 to simplify the labels that internet providers must show for their plans. The rule's effective date was September 14, 2026, yet the Federal Register notice delays the part of the rule that changes how labels are displayed. This article separates what is in effect from what is not.

## What is a broadband label?

A broadband label is a standardized summary of a home internet plan, similar to a food nutrition label. Congress directed the FCC in 2021 to require them. The FCC says providers must have a label for each standalone broadband plan they offer, showing introductory rates, data allowances and speeds, with links to information on network management practices and privacy policies. If you are shopping for a plan, the label is where to check the price after any promotional period; our guides to [buying your own router vs. ISP equipment]({{< relref "/guides/buy-your-own-router-vs-isp-equipment" >}}) and [how much internet speed you need]({{< relref "/guides/how-much-internet-speed-do-i-need" >}}) both point to it.

## What did the FCC decide on July 22, 2026?

The Commission adopted a Report and Order (FCC 26-48) with Chairman Carr and Commissioners Gomez and Trusty approving. The FCC describes it as making labels "more useful" for consumers and reducing compliance burdens on providers. According to the order and the Federal Register notice, the changes include:

- **Phone sales:** representatives can summarize the label conversationally instead of reading it word for word, but must cover price, speeds, latency, data allowance, contract term and early termination fees.
- **Websites and account portals:** a clearly labeled link or icon near the advertised plan may replace displaying the full label.
- **Location-based fees:** providers may show a single "up to" amount for government and third-party "passthrough" fees that vary by location, instead of itemizing each fee. (A passthrough fee is a monthly charge imposed by a government entity or third-party infrastructure owner that the provider passes on.) The order's example is "Up to $13.95 per month."
- **Removed items:** Affordable Connectivity Program (ACP) information, the separate machine-readable label file, and the requirement to keep archived labels for two years after a plan is withdrawn.
- **Kept:** labels must stay accessible to people with disabilities and appear in English and in any other language in which the provider markets its service.

## What is in effect today, and what is not?

Several outlets, including Broadband Breakfast, reported that these streamlined rules took effect September 14, 2026. The primary documents are more specific.

| Part of the rule | Status as of October 16, 2026 | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Section 8.1(b): definitions of "broadband internet access service" and "passthrough fee" | In effect since September 14, 2026 | Federal Register, instruction 2 |
| Section 8.1(a): amendments to paragraphs (a)(1) and (a)(2) and removal of (a)(3), (a)(5) and (a)(7), which is where the display changes sit | Delayed indefinitely; no effective date announced | Federal Register, instruction 3; order paragraph 61 |

The Federal Register notice says the rule is "Effective September 14, 2026, except for instruction 3 (Sec. 8.1(a)), which is delayed indefinitely." The order itself says the section 8.1(a) amendments "will not become effective until the Office of Management and Budget completes review of any information collection requirements" under the Paperwork Reduction Act, and directs the Consumer and Governmental Affairs Bureau to announce the date by Public Notice. We searched for that notice and did not find one as of October 16, 2026.

We could not map every individual change (for example, the ACP removal) to a specific paragraph from the sources we read. Where we describe a change as "not yet in effect," that follows from the Federal Register's instruction 3 and the order's paragraph 61, not from a line-by-line reading of each paragraph.

## What it means for you

- **Labels still exist, and the existing requirements still apply.** Providers must still have a label for each standalone plan.
- **Do not expect shorter phone pitches or link-only displays yet.** Those changes wait for the date the FCC will announce.
- **When they do take effect, a link or icon should lead to the plan's label.** The order requires it to sit near the advertised plan and go directly to that plan's label.
- **"Up to" fees would be a maximum, not your exact bill.** The order says a displayed figure must not understate current fees. Ask your provider for the total for your address.

## Bottom line

The FCC has decided to simplify broadband labels, but the display changes consumers would notice are not yet in effect. We will update this article when the FCC announces a date for the section 8.1(a) amendments.
