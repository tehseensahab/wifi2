The FCC's changes to broadband labels, the "nutrition label" for internet plans, are only partly in effect. The Federal Register says the rule took effect on September 14, 2026, but not the paragraph that actually changes the labels. That one is on hold, and the FCC has not said when it will start. If you are comparing plans today, expect the label rules that were already in place.

Short summaries can blur this distinction, so this article follows the primary documents: the FCC's order and the Federal Register notice.

## What did the FCC change, and when?

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| November 14, 2022 | FCC adopts the original label rules (order FCC 22-86) |
| April 10, 2024 | Labels become required at points of sale, per the FCC's news release that day |
| July 22, 2026 | FCC adopts revisions (order FCC 26-48); the full text is released July 23, 2026 |
| August 13, 2026 | Revisions published in the Federal Register (91 FR 52251) |
| September 14, 2026 | Stated effective date, with one exception (below) |
| Not announced | Effective date for the paragraph that rewrites the label rules, 47 CFR 8.1(a) |

The Federal Register's wording is: "Effective September 14, 2026, except for instruction 3 (Sec. 8.1(a)), which is delayed indefinitely. The Commission will publish a document in the Federal Register announcing the effective date."

The FCC's order explains why. Its amendments to section 8.1(a) "will not become effective until the Office of Management and Budget completes review of any information collection requirements" under the Paperwork Reduction Act, a law that requires federal review of new paperwork duties placed on businesses. A short list of what that leaves in place:

- **In effect since September 14:** the changes to section 8.1(b), which are two definitions. The first is the definition of "broadband internet access service." The second is the definition of "passthrough fee," explained below.
- **On hold:** everything in section 8.1(a), which is where the label content, display and phone rules live.

We checked the Federal Register and the FCC order. The FCC's own website blocked our automated access, so we could not read its label page directly. As of October 7, 2026, we found no announcement setting the date for section 8.1(a), but we cannot rule out that one has been posted. Check [the FCC's broadband label page](https://www.fcc.gov/broadbandlabels) for the latest.

Because the change to section 8.1(a) has not taken effect, the existing 8.1(a) rules presumably continue to apply. That is our inference from how the order is structured, not a statement the FCC makes in those words.

## What will change once section 8.1(a) takes effect?

The table compares the rules as the FCC's April 2024 release described them with the revisions in order FCC 26-48.

| Topic | Earlier rule | Revised rule, once effective |
|---|---|---|
| Display at a provider's website | The label itself, in close proximity to the plan's advertisement. The 2024 release said "not simply an icon or link" | The label, or an icon or link that connects directly to it, in close proximity to the advertised plan |
| Customer account portals | Label shown in the portal | The label, or an icon or link to it |
| Phone sales | Full label required at each point of sale | A representative may orally summarize the label; reading it word for word is not required |
| Location-based passthrough fees | Fees itemized | May be shown as a single total, either the exact monthly total or an "up to" maximum |
| Machine-readable label file | Required (a separately hosted spreadsheet-style data file) | Requirement eliminated |
| Two-year archive of labels after a plan is retired | Required | Requirement eliminated |
| Affordable Connectivity Program disclosure | Referenced on label | Removed; the program ended June 1, 2024 |

Two things do not change. Labels must remain accessible to people with disabilities, and they must be displayed in the language or languages the provider uses to market the service.

### The "up to" fee rule, in plain terms

A passthrough fee is a monthly charge that a government or third-party infrastructure owner imposes, that the provider chooses to list as a separate charge instead of building into its price, and that varies by customer location. These are the charges that can make a plan's real cost differ from its advertised price.

Under the revised rule, a provider that chooses the "up to" option must show:

1. One "up to" total for all passthrough fees.
2. An "up to" amount for government-imposed fees.
3. An "up to" amount for fees imposed by non-governmental third-party infrastructure owners, plus the types of fees included in each group.

The label must also point to a provider web page describing each type of fee. The FCC's order gives a hypothetical example: if fees never exceed $13.95 a month anywhere the plan is sold, the label could say "Up to $13.95 per month." That figure is the FCC's illustration, not a real provider's price.

For you, the "up to" number is a ceiling for your location, not a quote. Your own fees may be lower. Ask the provider for the exact figure at your address before you sign.

### What a phone representative must cover

When a plan is sold by phone, the revised rule says a provider satisfies the label requirement by orally summarizing: the monthly price including monthly fees (with any introductory rate and its duration), typical download and upload speeds, latency, data allowance, contract length if any, and early termination fees if any.

## What does a label show now?

The label requirements come from the 2022 order. Providers must create a label for each stand-alone home or mobile broadband plan they currently offer. According to the FCC's July 22, 2026 news release, labels must show introductory rates, data allowances and broadband speeds, and include links to network management and privacy policy information. The 2022 order adds that labels list:

- Recurring monthly charges beyond the base price, such as equipment rental.
- One-time fees at sign-up, such as a charge for buying a modem or router, an activation fee, a deposit, an installation fee, or an early termination fee.
- Typical upload and download speeds and typical latency.
- A statement that taxes will apply and vary by location. Taxes are not itemized.

"Typical" speeds describe what the provider reports customers usually experience, not a guarantee. For how to read those numbers against your household's needs, see [How Much Internet Speed Do I Need?]({{< relref "/guides/how-much-internet-speed-do-i-need" >}}).

## How should you use a label when shopping?

1. **Ask for the label for the exact plan.** Providers must have one for each stand-alone plan they offer. Online, look for it near the advertised plan.
2. **Check whether the price is introductory.** The label should state how long the rate lasts and what the price becomes afterward.
3. **Add up fees yourself.** Monthly equipment rental and any location-based fees raise the monthly cost above the base price. Taxes come on top.
4. **Look at one-time fees and early termination fees** before comparing the monthly price alone.
5. **Check the data allowance** and what happens when you exceed it.
6. **Compare technologies like for like.** Typical speeds and latency are reported per plan. Our guide to [fiber, cable and 5G home internet]({{< relref "/guides/fiber-vs-cable-vs-5g-home-internet" >}}) explains how the technologies differ.

## What we could not confirm

- **The effective date for section 8.1(a).** None had been announced in the sources we could read as of October 7, 2026.
- **How individual providers will implement the changes.** We did not survey provider websites, so we make no claim about which companies already use links or "up to" fees.
- **Anything the FCC may have published after the documents we read.** The Federal Register notice and the FCC order both separate section 8.1(a) from the rest, but a later public notice would change the picture.

## Bottom line

The FCC has decided to simplify broadband labels, but as of October 7, 2026, the part that changes what providers show you has not started. The Federal Register's September 14 date covers two definitions only. When the FCC announces the real start date, expect links and icons instead of full labels, an "up to" figure for location-based fees, and conversational phone summaries. Until then, ask for the label, add up the fees yourself, and get the exact price at your address in writing.
