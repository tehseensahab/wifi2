Two companies now sell Wi-Fi 8 routers, and neither sells one in the United States yet. TP-Link opened pre-orders for its Archer 8 Ultra on September 30, 2026 in the UK, Germany, Australia and Canada. ASUS put its ROG Rapture GT-BN98 on sale in Canada on September 4, 2026. As of October 3, 2026, neither company has announced a US on-sale date or a US price.

For US buyers there are two separate questions. The first is regulatory: which companies are allowed to bring a new router model to the US at all. The second is practical: whether a router built on a draft standard is worth buying when it does arrive.

*This article is research-based. WiFi Weekly has not tested any Wi-Fi 8 router. Specifications and performance figures below are manufacturer claims and are labeled as such.*

|---|---|---|
| Where it is sold | Pre-orders in the UK, Germany, Australia, Canada "and more" since September 30, 2026 | In Canadian retail stores since September 4, 2026; worldwide release scheduled for October 2026 |

| Claimed Wi-Fi speed | Up to 19 Gbps across three bands (manufacturer figure, combined across bands) | Up to 30,000 Mbps on the Pro model only (manufacturer figure) |

Sources: TP-Link press release (September 30, 2026), ASUS press release (September 4, 2026), FCC Covered List page (updated October 2, 2026).

The speed figures add up the maximum link rate of every band. No single phone or laptop connects at that rate. TP-Link's own footnote says its figures are maximum "up to" values from internal testing under controlled conditions and that actual performance varies.

Neither press release lists a price. TechRadar reported a UK pre-order price of £669.99 on September 30, 2026, which it said would later rise to £899.99, and an Australian price of AU$1,499.99. UK and Australian shelf prices normally include sales tax, so they don't convert directly into a US price.

## Why can't US buyers order one?

On March 23, 2026, the Federal Communications Commission (FCC) added "routers produced in a foreign country" to its Covered List. The Covered List is the FCC's list of equipment considered a national security risk. Equipment on it cannot receive FCC equipment authorization, and most electronic devices need that authorization before they can be imported or sold in the US.

The FCC's own guidance spells out what that means:

- **New models** of foreign-produced consumer routers cannot be authorized, so they cannot be imported or sold in the US, unless they have a Conditional Approval.
- **Models authorized before the rule** can still be imported, sold and used.
- **Routers you already own** are unaffected. The FCC says consumers "do not need to do anything."

"Produced in a foreign country" is broad. The FCC says it covers any major stage of production, including manufacturing, assembly, design and development, and that the nationality of the company does not matter. A router designed in the US and built overseas is covered.

A Conditional Approval is the exemption. It comes from the Department of War or the Department of Homeland Security, and the FCC publishes each one. The FCC's list was last updated on October 2, 2026. For the brands most US shoppers know, it shows:

| Company | Router lines covered | Approval window |
|---|---|---|
| Netgear | Nighthawk and Orbi routers, plus cable gateways and cable modems | April 14, 2026 to October 1, 2027 |
| eero (Amazon) | eero, eero Pro, eero Max, eero PoE, eero Outdoor, eero Signal, Amazon Leo routers | April 22, 2026 to October 31, 2027 |
| ASUS | RT, ROG GT, ROG Strix GS, TUF Gaming, ExpertWiFi and ZenWiFi Mesh series, 5G MiFi, repeaters and access points | September 9, 2026 to March 6, 2028 |
| TP-Link | Not on the list | None |

Source: FCC Covered List page, Conditional Approvals tab, updated October 2, 2026. The full list has more than 20 router entries, most of them equipment supplied through internet providers.

Two limits on what this table tells you:

1. **A Conditional Approval is not a product launch.** It lets a company apply for FCC equipment authorization for new models during the window. ASUS's approval names the ROG GT and ZenWiFi Mesh series, and its announced Wi-Fi 8 models (ROG Rapture GT-BN98, ZenWiFi BN12) carry those family names. ASUS has not announced a US date.
2. **TP-Link's absence from the list is the only thing the FCC record shows.** It does not say whether TP-Link has applied or what the status of any application is. TP-Link has not given a reason for the missing US date in its press releases.

The same FCC list also shows Wi-Fi 8 equipment from companies that mainly supply internet providers: a Nokia Wi-Fi 8 router (approved May 15, 2026) and two Askey Wi-Fi 8 gateways (approved July 24, 2026). So the first Wi-Fi 8 box in some US homes may come from an ISP, not a store shelf. No ISP launch date is given in the FCC record.

## Can I just import one?

The FCC's guidance addresses this directly. For covered routers that have not received FCC equipment authorization, it says "the general answer is 'no'" to using them in the US. Routers that already carry an FCC ID are not restricted, wherever you bought them.

There are practical problems too. A router sold for another country is set up for that country's radio rules, and you would be relying on overseas warranty support. We don't recommend it.

## What does Wi-Fi 8 change?

Wi-Fi 8 is the marketing name for IEEE 802.11bn, a standard still being written by the Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers (IEEE). The project's formal title is "Enhancements for Ultra High Reliability," and that is the point of it.

The IEEE project document sets three targets, each compared with Wi-Fi 7. The standard must include at least one mode that can:

- increase throughput by 25%,
- reduce latency by 25% at the 95th percentile, meaning the slow moments, not the average,
- reduce lost data packets by 25%.

These are design targets under conditions the project defines. They are not promised results in your house.

What Wi-Fi 8 does not do is raise the top speed. According to MediaTek figures reported by PCWorld in December 2024, Wi-Fi 8 keeps the same maximum channel width (320 MHz), the same modulation and the same three bands (2.4, 5 and 6 GHz) as Wi-Fi 7, for the same theoretical peak rate. Those figures describe a draft and could change before the standard is final.

The new features are about how routers behave when conditions are bad: several access points coordinating so they don't talk over each other, better performance at the edge of coverage, and smoother handoff as you move between mesh units. That makes Wi-Fi 8 more relevant to mesh systems and crowded apartment buildings than to a single router in a small home.

## How finished is the standard?

It is a draft. The IEEE task group's status page lists the second draft (D2.0) as complete and projects final approval by the IEEE Standards Board in May 2028. That date is a projection, and standards schedules can slip.

Routers shipping now are built to the draft. TP-Link's September 30 release splits the Archer 8 Ultra's Wi-Fi 8 features into two groups:

- **At launch:** UEQM, new MCS rates, 2×LDPC and NPCA (features aimed at steadier links and getting around busy channels).
- **Later, by firmware update:** DSO, ELR, DRU, Co-TDMA, Co-SR and Co-BF. This group includes the coordination features that most define Wi-Fi 8. TP-Link gives no date for the update.

There is also no certification program yet. The Wi-Fi Alliance, the industry group that tests products for interoperability, has not launched one for Wi-Fi 8. Industry outlet Wi-Fi NOW reported in June 2026 that the program is expected to launch in January 2028. Until then, a "Wi-Fi 8" label is the manufacturer's own claim.

## Should you wait, or buy Wi-Fi 7 or Wi-Fi 6E now?

There is nothing to decide between today in the US, because there is no Wi-Fi 8 router to buy. The useful question is whether to delay a purchase you were already planning.

| Your situation | Reasonable choice | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Your current Wi-Fi works | Keep it | Nothing in Wi-Fi 8 fixes a problem you don't have |
| You need better coverage now | Buy a current Wi-Fi 6E or Wi-Fi 7 system | Placement and wired links between units matter more than the Wi-Fi generation |
| You want Wi-Fi 8 specifically | Wait for US launches, and ideally for certified products | No US price or date yet; key features arrive later by firmware; no certification program |
| You're tempted to import | Don't | FCC guidance says unauthorized routers generally can't be used in the US |

One more reason not to hurry: a router's new features only help devices that support them. TechRadar reported on September 30, 2026 that Wi-Fi 8 phones and laptops are not expected until 2027. ASUS says its Wi-Fi 8 router delivers "measurable performance gains" with Wi-Fi 7 devices. That is a manufacturer claim that we have not tested and have not seen independently measured.

## Bottom line

Wi-Fi 8 routers exist, but not for US shoppers yet. Based on the FCC's list as of October 2, 2026, ASUS is cleared to seek authorization for new models and TP-Link is not on the list. Even when a US launch comes, you would be buying a draft-standard router whose main features arrive later and whose benefits depend on devices that aren't on sale. If your network needs help today, fix placement and coverage with current gear. We'll update this article when a US price and date are announced.
