The best mesh system for a large house depends less on the Wi-Fi generation than on whether you can run Ethernet cable between the units. With wiring, even a budget system can perform well; without it, you need a tri-band system with a strong wireless link between units. Distance tests also show that rankings flip as you move away from the router, so a system that wins at 15 feet can lose at 90.

This guide is for people with a big or multi-story house who already know a single router isn't enough. If you're not sure, [start with whether you need a router or mesh at all]({{< relref "/guides/wifi-router-vs-mesh" >}}).

*WiFi Weekly has not physically tested the products below. Performance figures come from the named independent reviewers, and specifications come from manufacturers.*

## What matters most when choosing mesh for a big house?

Four things, in this order.

**Backhaul.** This is the connection between mesh units. If you can connect units with Ethernet, the system doesn't need to spend radio capacity relaying your traffic. If you can't, the quality of the wireless link becomes the deciding factor.

**Reach at the far end.** In a large house, the number that matters is how a unit performs in the room farthest from it, not how fast it is beside the router.

**Ports.** A 2.5 Gbps or faster port lets a wired backhaul and a fast internet plan use their full speed.

**Costs after purchase.** Some brands put advanced security features behind subscriptions. Tom's Guide notes that the Orbi 870 includes only 90 days of Netgear's Armor security and technical support.

Three things stand out.

First, the 15-foot column barely separates these systems, and it's the number that matters least in a big house. Nobody's phone sits next to the router all day.

Second, the ranking flips with distance. The eero Pro 7 was fastest at 50 feet and slowest at 90. The cheap dual-band Deco BE23 was the fastest at 75 and 90 feet. The Orbi 870 held about 628 Mbps from 50 to 75 feet, which is why Tom's Guide picked it as its range pick.

Third, a big house is really a placement problem. Most systems here delivered several hundred megabits at 50 feet and fell off sharply after 75. The goal is to place units so no room is much farther than that from a unit.

## Wired or wireless backhaul: which matters more?

Wired, and it isn't close. A cable between units gives each one a dedicated path to the router, so the far end of the house isn't competing with your phone for airtime.

If your house already has Ethernet jacks in the walls, use them. If it doesn't, coax outlets may offer another route: MoCA adapters carry Ethernet over the coax cable already in many U.S. homes. Check whether your cable provider's TV equipment already uses that coax before adding adapters.

If you can't wire anything, shop for a tri-band system, and treat range and wireless-backhaul reviews as the key evidence. Two cautions from independent reviewers:

- Dong Knows Tech found the Orbi 770 is tri-band without a permanently dedicated backhaul band, which he considered adequate given how Wi-Fi 7 manages traffic across bands.
- Dong Knows Tech reported that the ASUS ZenWiFi BT10 struggled with stable wireless backhaul in its early 2024 firmware, and the wired setup performed well.

## Which mesh system fits which large house?

There's no single best pick, so here they are by situation.

### You can run Ethernet and want to spend less: TP-Link Deco BE23

Tom's Guide calls the Deco BE23 the best budget pick, and its 75- and 90-foot numbers above beat several far pricier systems. It's dual-band, so it has no 6 GHz radio, and each unit has two 2.5 Gbps ports. Tom's Guide says a three-pack can cost as low as $180.

**Skip it if** you need wireless backhaul in a house with thick walls, since a dual-band system shares one band between backhaul and your devices. Also see the FCC note below.

### You can't run cable and want strong range: Netgear Orbi 870 or Orbi 770

Tom's Guide picks the Orbi 870 for range and praises its mid- and long-distance speeds, along with a 10 Gbps input and four 2.5 Gbps LAN ports. The catch is price: $1,000 for a two-pack.

The Orbi 770 is Netgear's lower-cost Wi-Fi 7 tri-band. Netgear says a three-pack covers up to 8,000 sq ft, each satellite adds up to 2,750 sq ft, and each unit has 2.5 Gbps Ethernet that can serve as wired backhaul. In Tom's Guide's 2024 review, it had a 95-foot range and came close to covering a 3,500 sq ft home.

**Skip it if** you need 10 Gbps ports (the Orbi 770 has none) or you're unwilling to pay for Netgear's optional security subscription after the trial period. Coverage claims are lab maximums; Netgear itself says building construction lowers them.

### You want the simplest setup: eero Pro 7

Tom's Guide picked the Pro 7 for simplicity and praised its two 5 Gbps ports and strong mid-range speeds. Its 20.8 Mbps result at 90 feet is the reason to buy a three-pack and space units closer together rather than stretching two units across a big house. The eero Plus subscription, reported at $99.99 a year in 2025, is optional but gates advanced features. Confirm the current price on eero.com.

**Skip it if** you want deep settings control or more than two Ethernet ports per unit.

### You have multi-gig internet and wired gear: ASUS ZenWiFi BT10

ASUS lists it as a tri-band Wi-Fi 7 system with dual 10 Gbps ports on each unit. HighSpeedInternet.com found its security tools and parental controls don't need a subscription. Tom's Hardware called it fast on multi-gig connections but pricey. ASUS's AiMesh software also lets you add compatible ASUS routers to the same mesh.

**Skip it if** you want a minimal, app-only experience, or if you're relying on wireless backhaul.

### Other systems Tom's Guide tested

Tom's Guide's top overall pick is the MSI Roamii BE Pro, which it sells only in two-packs (so adding a third node means buying another two-pack). The Acer Predator Connect T7 targets gamers and costs about $330 per unit with no multi-pack discount, according to Tom's Guide. We could not confirm the FCC status for either brand, so check before buying.

## How many nodes do I need, and where do they go?

Tom's Guide's rule of thumb: a two-pack suits a small or medium house, and a three-pack suits homes with more than three bedrooms or several floors. You can start with two and add one later.

Say your router is in a downstairs office at the front and the main bedroom upstairs at the back gets one bar. Don't put the second unit in the bedroom. It needs a strong signal from the first unit to pass anything on. Put it about halfway, like an upstairs hallway near the stairs, so it has a good link back and still reaches the bedroom.

Some rules of thumb:

- Keep each unit in the open and off the floor, not in a cabinet or behind a TV.
- Add nodes to fix a dead spot, not to fill every room. Nodes crowded together don't help.
- If a dead spot remains after moving nodes, try wiring that unit before buying a bigger system.

## How does the FCC router rule affect this purchase?

On March 23, 2026 the FCC added consumer routers produced in foreign countries to its Covered List, which blocks new models from receiving FCC authorization. Models already authorized can still be sold, and routers you own are unaffected. The FCC also moved the software-update cutoff for covered routers to January 1, 2029, according to CEPRO.

Companies can request Conditional Approvals. Netgear and eero were approved in April 2026, and ASUS in early September, based on press reports. TP-Link had not been approved as of mid-September reporting. For this guide, that means the Deco BE23 and BE85 remain legal to buy and use, but TP-Link cannot introduce new models until it gets approval. Check the FCC's list before buying. Our large-house router guide covers this in more detail.

## When is mesh the wrong answer?

If your home is under about 2,500 square feet, one story and open, one good router placed near the center often does the job. If Wi-Fi is slow everywhere, including right next to the router, the problem may be your internet plan, modem or ISP, and mesh won't fix it. Test with a wired connection before spending anything.

## Bottom line

For a large house, decide about wiring first. If you can run Ethernet, a budget system like the Deco BE23 performed well in Tom's Guide's distance tests. If you can't, a tri-band system such as the Orbi 770 or 870 is the safer choice. If you have multi-gig internet and wired gear, the ASUS ZenWiFi BT10's dual 10 Gbps ports make sense. Choose eero Pro 7 for simple setup, but buy three units rather than two for a big house. Check the FCC's approval list on the day you buy.
