The best outdoor mesh Wi-Fi unit is the one made for the system you already have indoors. eero's and TP-Link's outdoor nodes mesh only with their own brand's systems, so an eero home should look at the **eero Outdoor 7**, a TP-Link Deco home at the **Deco BE25-Outdoor** (or the older, Wi-Fi 6 **Deco X50-Outdoor**), and a Ubiquiti UniFi network at the **UniFi U7 Outdoor**. All of them are dual-band, none uses 6 GHz, and all of them can run on one PoE+ Ethernet cable that carries both power and data to the unit.

This is a research-based guide. We haven't tested these units ourselves: the specs, coverage figures and limits below come from eero, TP-Link and Ubiquiti, and prices were checked on November 4, 2026. Speeds are each maker's maximum link rates, not the throughput you'd see in a yard.

## First, check whether you need an outdoor unit at all

An outdoor node needs a mounting spot, a cable run or a nearby indoor unit, and in some cases a PoE switch or injector. Before buying one, try moving an indoor unit. eero's own advice for outdoor coverage is to place an eero near the exterior wall closest to the outdoor space. If your patio is just outside a wall that already has a mesh node behind it, that may be enough.

Don't put an indoor unit outside to save money. eero says "most eeros aren't waterproof or water resistant," and unless an indoor unit's maker gives it a weather rating, assume it isn't built for rain, dust or summer heat.

An outdoor unit makes sense when the area you want to cover is far from the house (a detached garage, a pool, the back of a large lot), or when there's no indoor spot close enough to the outside wall. Our guide to [finding and fixing Wi-Fi dead zones]({{< relref "/guides/find-and-fix-wifi-dead-zones" >}}) covers how to confirm where the signal actually drops off.

## eero Outdoor 7: for eero homes

eero says most eeros aren't waterproof or water resistant; the Outdoor 7 is its weather-rated model. eero rates it IP66 and says it "can safely operate in temperature ranges of -40°F to 131°F (-40°C to 55°C)."

What to know before you buy:

- **It only works with eero.** eero says the Outdoor 7 "is designed to work exclusively with other eero wifi products" and, because it uses eero's TrueMesh technology, "is incompatible with non-eero wifi routers and access points."
- **It works with older eeros, with a catch.** eero says it's "backwards compatible with all eero hardware," but pairing it with non-Wi-Fi 7 eeros "may result in a reduction of performance across the network." eero recommends using it with other Wi-Fi 7 eeros. Our [eero 7 vs. eero Pro 7 vs. eero Max 7]({{< relref "/guides/eero-7-vs-eero-pro-7-vs-eero-max-7" >}}) comparison covers the indoor models.
- **No 6 GHz.** eero's FAQ says the Outdoor 7 "does not use the 6 GHz band, and is a dual-band router." It has 2x2 radios on 2.4 GHz and 5 GHz, and eero lists wireless speeds "up to 2.1 Gbps," a theoretical maximum, not real-world speed.
- **Power is PoE+ or nothing.** eero says it "requires a PoE+ (802.3at) connection or better" and that standard PoE (802.3af) "will not provide a large enough power budget." You don't need an eero PoE Gateway; eero says any PoE+ switch or injector with a Cat5e or better cable works, and it also sells an outdoor PoE+ power adapter that plugs into an AC outlet. eero's FAQ answers a question from buyers whose Outdoor 7 "didn't come with a power supply," so check which package you're buying.
- **One Ethernet port.** It has a single 2.5 GbE PoE+ port. If you want to wire other outdoor gear to it, you'll need a switch that's protected from the weather.
- **Mounting rules.** eero says it "must be mounted vertically on a wall or post with the bracket provided," and warns it "is subject to certain signal issues when operating near or on metal surfaces."

eero's coverage claim is "up to 15,000 sq. ft." That's eero's own outdoor-coverage claim; walls, trees, terrain and where you mount it all cut into it.

**Price:** eero.com listed it "starting at $399.99" on November 4, 2026. That's about double the UniFi U7 Outdoor, but the UniFi unit can't mesh with eeros.

## TP-Link Deco BE25-Outdoor: for Deco homes

TP-Link says its outdoor Decos are "compatible with every other Deco model to form a Mesh network," so either outdoor model can join an existing Deco system of any generation.

The Deco BE25-Outdoor is the Wi-Fi 7 model. TP-Link's US specs list:

- **Wi-Fi 7 on 2.4 GHz and 5 GHz,** with maximum link rates of 4324 Mbps (5 GHz) and 688 Mbps (2.4 GHz). There's no 6 GHz radio.
- **IP65** dust and water resistance and an operating range of -22°F to 140°F (-30°C to 60°C).
- **Two 2.5 Gbps Ethernet ports,** auto-sensing for WAN or LAN, with PoE input. The second port is the practical difference from the eero: you can wire another device straight to it (TP-Link doesn't say it supplies PoE power).
- **Power from 802.3at PoE or an AC adapter.**
- **Router or access point mode,** so it can also serve as the main unit of a Deco network, for example in a detached building with its own connection.
- **Pole, wall or tabletop** mounting.
- **Coverage "up to 2,800 ft²"** per unit, by TP-Link's figure.

### Deco X50-Outdoor: the Wi-Fi 6 option

The older Deco X50-Outdoor has the same IP65 rating, temperature range, PoE-or-AC power and broad Deco compatibility, but it's Wi-Fi 6, with link rates of 2402 Mbps (5 GHz) and 574 Mbps (2.4 GHz), two Gigabit (not 2.5 Gbps) ports and a 2,500 ft² coverage figure. It makes sense only if you find it for noticeably less than the BE25-Outdoor and your internet plan is under a gigabit.

TP-Link's X50-Outdoor FAQ is written for the X50, but it's a useful guide for the BE25 too:

- **Keep wireless links short.** TP-Link says to "keep the distance between X50-Outdoor and the front-end Deco within 33 feet (or 10 meters)," and no more than 50 feet. That limit doesn't apply to a wired connection.
- **PoE carries data too.** TP-Link confirms Ethernet backhaul still works when the unit is powered by PoE.
- **It's weather resistant, not waterproof.** TP-Link says IP65 means protection "from the intrusion of sprayed water," and that the unit can't be used underwater. For hurricanes, it says you "will need additional reinforcement" against high winds.
- **Mount it at up to 25 feet.** The recommended installation height is 0 to 8 m (0 to 25 feet).

**Price:** We didn't verify a US price for either Deco outdoor model today, so we've left prices out. Compare the BE25-Outdoor and X50-Outdoor at the time you buy.

## UniFi U7 Outdoor: for UniFi networks

The UniFi U7 Outdoor is an outdoor access point, not a consumer mesh node. It fits homes that already run Ubiquiti UniFi gear. Ubiquiti's help center says adoption links a device "to the UniFi Gateway and the UniFi application that will manage it," and wired access points need a UniFi host, such as a UniFi gateway, a CloudKey or a self-hosted server, running UniFi Network.

Ubiquiti describes it as an "All-weather WiFi 7 AP" with 4 spatial streams and an "integrated directional super antenna." Its outdoor comparison page lists 2.4 GHz and 5 GHz, a 2.5 GbE uplink, PoE+ power and an IPX6 rating, with optional external omni antennas included. Ubiquiti's coverage figure is 5,000 ft² with the directional antenna.

The directional antenna is the reason to pick it: it aims coverage at one area, such as a yard seen from the back wall of the house, rather than spreading it in every direction.

**Price:** $199 at the Ubiquiti US store on November 4, 2026, shown as $219 with the store's listed surcharge.

## If your indoor Wi-Fi is another brand

You have three choices:

1. **Wire an outdoor access point.** An outdoor access point connected to your router by Ethernet doesn't need to mesh with your indoor system. It will be managed separately from your indoor system, as its own network or access point. Our guide to [access points vs. extenders vs. mesh]({{< relref "/guides/access-point-vs-extender-vs-mesh" >}}) explains the trade-offs.
2. **Move an indoor unit closer to the outside wall,** as above. It costs nothing to try.
3. **Replace the indoor system** with one whose maker sells an outdoor node. That only makes sense if the indoor system is due for replacement anyway.

This guide doesn't cover NETGEAR Orbi; if you own an Orbi system, check NETGEAR's site for an outdoor satellite that supports your model.

## How to set up an outdoor unit so it actually works

- **Run Ethernet if you can.** All of these units take PoE+ on one cable, which powers the unit and carries the connection back to your network. A wired link isn't limited by distance the way TP-Link's 33-to-50-foot wireless guidance is, and it leaves the unit's radios free for your devices. Our [wired vs. wireless backhaul guide]({{< relref "/guides/mesh-wifi-wired-vs-wireless-backhaul" >}}) explains why.
- **Buy PoE+, not PoE.** If you're adding an injector or switch, make sure it's 802.3at (PoE+) or better. eero is explicit that 802.3af won't work.
- **Keep it away from metal and indoor nodes.** eero warns about metal surfaces and suggests placing the Outdoor 7 "further away from the rest of the indoor eero units" to reduce overlapping signals.
- **Seal the cable entry.** These units are rated against water jets (the eero and Decos against dust too; the U7's IPX6 rating doesn't cover dust), but the hole you drill for the cable isn't. Use a drip loop and seal the wall penetration.
- **Expect 2.4 GHz to reach farthest.** With no 6 GHz on any of these units, devices at the far edge of the yard will often end up on 2.4 GHz, which is slower but goes further.

## Bottom line

Start with the brand you already own. For eero, the Outdoor 7 is eero's outdoor model and it's expensive, but it's built to mesh with the rest of your eeros. For Deco, the BE25-Outdoor is the better buy over the X50-Outdoor unless the older model is much cheaper. For UniFi, the U7 Outdoor costs about half as much as the eero and aims its signal where you point it. Whichever you choose, plan the PoE+ cable run before you plan the mounting spot.
