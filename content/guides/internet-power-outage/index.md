**No: home internet doesn't keep working in a power outage on its own, whether it's cable, fiber or 5G.** Cable, fiber and 5G home internet all depend on a box in your home (a modem, a fiber ONT or a 5G gateway) plus a router, and all of them need electricity. If you keep that equipment running with a UPS or generator, your internet can stay up, but only as long as your provider's network outside the house still has power. The battery backups that providers sell for home phone service usually power only the phone line, not internet.

This guide is based on Verizon, Xfinity/Comcast and T-Mobile support pages and announcements, an FCC storm report, the FCC's backup power rules, Fairfax County's backup power guide and APC's UPS runtime chart. It covers what to expect by connection type and what actually keeps you online. For sizing a battery for your own gear, see our [best UPS for a router and modem](/guides/best-ups-for-router/) guide.

## Why the internet goes down when the power does

Two things have to stay powered for you to get online:

1. **Your equipment.** The modem, ONT or gateway, plus your router and any mesh units. When the outlet goes dead, Wi-Fi goes with it.
2. **Your provider's network.** The equipment between your home and the internet also needs power. Comcast, for example, says its network is built to run for up to four hours without commercial power.

A UPS solves the first problem. Nothing you can buy solves the second, which is why a backup connection that uses different infrastructure matters if you need to stay online through long outages.

## Cable internet (Xfinity, Spectrum, Cox)

Your cable modem or gateway needs wall power; Fairfax County's guide puts it plainly: "Internet service you receive over coaxial, or fiber optic cables will not work" without power for your equipment. On the network side, Comcast said in a September 2025 announcement that its "network is already built to stay online for up to four hours without commercial power," and that "most power outages are resolved in less than two hours and impact fewer than 200 customer locations at a time." That's Comcast's own claim for its network; other cable providers' backup power varies.

By Comcast's figures, a modem and router on a UPS may keep you online through a short local outage. During an outage longer than the network's backup, service can drop even if your own equipment is powered.

**What the voice battery does.** Xfinity sells a battery backup for its Voice modem. Its support article says a Battery Backup "can support your service for several hours" and that your phone and alarm "should remain fully operational for up to 24 hours"; only your primary phone number works on battery. The article covers phone and alarm service only and doesn't say the battery keeps internet or Wi-Fi running.

**Storm-Ready WiFi.** Comcast also sells a separate device for this problem. In its August 15, 2023 launch announcement, Comcast said Storm-Ready WiFi has a backup battery that lasts "up to 4 hours" and "automatically transitions to free unlimited cellular data during outages," with speeds "limited to those then offered through the available LTE cellular service." It launched at $7 a month for 36 months; check Xfinity for current pricing and eligibility.

## Fiber internet (Verizon Fios and others)

Fiber needs an ONT (optical network terminal) in your home to turn the light signal into an Ethernet connection, and the ONT needs power. Verizon's support page says, "Your ONT requires electricity to operate all Verizon Fios services."

Verizon's Fios voice battery backup doesn't change that for internet. Verizon says, "A fully charged 12V battery provides up to 8 hours of backup power for voice services, including 911 calls," and "Backup power does not support Internet or TV service during an outage."

To keep Fios internet running, the ONT and your router both need backup power, such as a UPS sized for both. Our [modem vs. router vs. gateway explainer](/guides/modem-vs-router-vs-gateway/) covers what each box does.

## 5G home internet (T-Mobile, Verizon, AT&T)

5G home internet replaces the cable or fiber line with a cellular connection, but the gateway still plugs into the wall. T-Mobile says, "Your 5G gateway needs power to work," and that it "can stay connected only if the gateway is plugged into a battery backup" or something "like a home generator or other alternative power source."

The cell site that serves your gateway also needs power. In the FCC's January 29, 2026 status report on Winter Storm Fern, 2.7% of cell sites in the affected area were out of service. In Mississippi, 63 of 718 sites were out, and the report attributed 53 of those 63 to power and 9 to transport (the links connecting the site). The same report counted 70,355 cable and wireline subscribers out of service in the disaster area.

A major winter storm is an extreme case, but the report shows that a wireless connection isn't immune to power problems.

## What about landlines and phone backup rules?

Phone service delivered over cable or fiber isn't powered by the provider the way traditional copper landlines were. In 2015, the FCC required providers of "facilities-based, fixed voice service that is not line-powered" to offer at least one backup power option "capable of supporting at least 8 hours of uninterrupted 911 service," and later a 24-hour option. Those rules are about voice and 911 calls, not internet, which helps explain why provider batteries are sized and sold for the phone line.

Fairfax County, Virginia's cable consumer office makes the same point in its backup power guide: VoIP phone services "are not powered from the network," so they need a battery backup unit, and it says home backup units "typically last about four to eight hours."

## How to stay online during an outage

1. **Put the modem or ONT and router on a UPS.** Everything your connection passes through needs power, including any mesh unit or switch between the modem and your devices. Our [best UPS for a router and modem](/guides/best-ups-for-router/) guide covers sizing and runtimes.
2. **Have a second connection that doesn't share the same wires.** A phone hotspot, a router that can use your [phone as backup internet](/guides/phone-backup-internet-router/), or a cellular backup device can cover a cable or fiber outage, as long as the nearby cell site has power.
3. **Keep phones and a battery bank charged** before a storm, so a hotspot is available if your home line goes down.
4. **Know what your provider's battery covers.** A voice battery from Verizon or Xfinity is sold for the phone line (and, for Xfinity, alarm service). Verizon says its battery doesn't support internet, and Xfinity's article doesn't mention internet.
5. **Expect less on backup.** Comcast says Storm-Ready WiFi speeds are limited to what the local LTE service offers, and a phone hotspot is shared by every device that joins it.

If you're still choosing a provider and outages are common where you live, our [fiber vs. cable vs. 5G home internet](/guides/fiber-vs-cable-vs-5g-home-internet/) comparison covers the other trade-offs.
