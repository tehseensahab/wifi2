Wi-Fi 6E is Wi-Fi 6 with one addition: access to the 6 GHz band. It doesn't raise the top speed. What it adds is a wide, uncrowded band that only newer devices can use. That helps most in apartments and busy homes, for 6E-capable phones and laptops close to the router. If your devices can't use 6 GHz, or your real problem is weak signal in far rooms, Wi-Fi 6 does the job.

This is a research-based comparison built from Wi-Fi Alliance, FCC and manufacturer documentation. We have not tested these routers ourselves.

## What is the difference between Wi-Fi 6 and Wi-Fi 6E?

Both use the same underlying standard, IEEE 802.11ax, and the same features. The Wi-Fi Alliance, the industry group that certifies Wi-Fi products, lists them for Wi-Fi 6: 160 MHz channels, 1024 QAM encoding, OFDMA (which shares a channel among several devices at once) and MU-MIMO (which sends data to several devices at the same time).

The Wi-Fi Alliance began certifying Wi-Fi 6E on January 7, 2021, describing it as extending "into the 6 GHz band the capabilities of Wi-Fi CERTIFIED 6." In the United States, an FCC rule effective July 27, 2020 made about 1,200 MHz of 6 GHz spectrum available for this kind of use, and the Wi-Fi Alliance says that room holds up to seven 160 MHz channels.

| | Wi-Fi 6 | Wi-Fi 6E |
|---|---|---|
| Standard | 802.11ax | 802.11ax |
| Bands | 2.4 GHz and 5 GHz | 2.4 GHz, 5 GHz and 6 GHz |
| Widest channel | 160 MHz | 160 MHz |
| Maximum link rate, two-antenna laptop (Intel) | 2.4 Gbps | 2.4 Gbps |
| Who else is on the extra band | Not applicable | Only Wi-Fi 6E and newer devices |
| Security on 6 GHz | Not applicable | WPA3 required |

The Intel figure is a theoretical radio rate between a laptop and the router, not an internet speed. It is the same for both, which is the point: Wi-Fi 6E's advantage is space, not a higher ceiling.

## When does the 6 GHz band actually help?

Three conditions have to line up.

**Your devices support it.** Netgear says 6 GHz is "exclusive to devices that support WiFi 6E and newer standards." On Windows, Netgear says PCs "must use the latest Windows 11 operating system release" and a 6 GHz-capable adapter with current drivers; it says Microsoft is not adding 6 GHz support to Windows 10. Apple's list of devices that can join Wi-Fi 6E or Wi-Fi 7 networks starts at iPhone 15 Pro and iPhone 15 Pro Max (excluding iPhone 15, iPhone 15 Plus, iPhone 16e and iPhone 17e) and Mac models from 2023, such as MacBook Pro, or 2024 for MacBook Air.

**The 5 GHz band is crowded.** Netgear says 6 GHz has less interference "since it has more channels and fewer devices." If you live in an apartment building with dozens of neighboring networks, or your home has many active devices, moving the newest ones to 6 GHz frees space for everything else. In a detached house with few neighbors, 5 GHz may already be uncrowded.

**The device is close to the router.** Netgear says 6 GHz "provides the least coverage" of the three bands but is the fastest. It works best in the same room or the next one. Our [Wi-Fi bands guide]({{< relref "/guides/2-4-ghz-vs-5-ghz-vs-6-ghz-wifi" >}}) explains why.

## When is Wi-Fi 6 enough?

- **Most of your devices don't support Wi-Fi 6E.** They will never use 6 GHz.
- **Your problem is coverage, not congestion.** A slow far bedroom is a range problem, and 6 GHz has the shortest range. Mesh units and placement fix that; see [router vs. mesh]({{< relref "/guides/wifi-router-vs-mesh" >}}) and [how to fix slow Wi-Fi]({{< relref "/guides/how-to-fix-slow-wifi" >}}).
- **Your internet plan is modest.** Neither generation raises your plan speed. See [how much internet speed you need]({{< relref "/guides/how-much-internet-speed-do-i-need" >}}).
- **You use Windows 10 PCs** that can't move to Windows 11.

## Which should I buy?

| Your situation | Reasonable choice | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Apartment or busy home, with recent phones and laptops | Wi-Fi 6E or newer | Those devices can use the uncrowded 6 GHz band |
| Mostly older devices | Wi-Fi 6 | They can't use 6 GHz |
| Large house with weak far rooms | Mesh, of either generation | 6 GHz has the shortest range; coverage matters more |
| Buying new and want the longest useful life | Compare against Wi-Fi 7 with a 6 GHz radio | Wi-Fi 7 includes 6 GHz and adds features for Wi-Fi 7 devices |
| Existing Wi-Fi 6 router works and still gets updates | Keep it | Upgrade when devices or congestion give you a reason |

We have not included prices; we only publish a price with the date we checked it. If you are weighing Wi-Fi 6E against Wi-Fi 7, see our [Wi-Fi 6E vs. Wi-Fi 7 guide]({{< relref "/guides/wifi-6e-vs-wifi-7" >}}).

## What should I check on the box?

- **"6 GHz" listed in the specifications.** That is what makes a router Wi-Fi 6E. Don't rely on the word "tri-band" alone; check that 6 GHz is one of the bands named.
- **WPA3 support.** Cisco's WPA3 guide states that "WPA3 is mandatory for all Wi-Fi 6E devices operating in 6 GHz band" and that "WPA2 is not permitted in 6 GHz operation." Set the router to WPA3, or WPA2/WPA3 transitional mode if older devices need it; see [WPA2 vs. WPA3]({{< relref "/guides/wpa2-vs-wpa3" >}}).
- **Firmware support.** Check how long the maker supports its routers with security updates; see [how to tell if your router still gets security updates]({{< relref "/guides/is-my-router-still-getting-security-updates" >}}).
- **Port speeds.** A fast Wi-Fi link is limited by a 1 Gbps internet port if your plan is faster than that.

## Bottom line

Wi-Fi 6E doesn't make Wi-Fi faster on paper. It gives newer devices a private lane. Pay for it if you have 6E-capable devices and a crowded 5 GHz band, and keep those devices near the router. If your devices are older or your problem is reach, Wi-Fi 6 with good placement, or mesh, is the better use of the money.
