The broadband label is a standardized fact sheet that US internet providers must publish for each plan they sell. To use it well, read three lines first: the price after any introductory rate ends, the fees, and the contract terms. Then check the typical speeds and latency against what your household does online.

This guide is based on the FCC's own order and the Federal Register notice. We have not audited any provider's labels, and every example price below is hypothetical.

## What is a broadband label?

Congress required the labels in the 2021 Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act. The law told the Federal Communications Commission (FCC), the US communications regulator, to require labels showing "whether the offered price is an introductory rate and, if so, the price the consumer will be required to pay following the introductory period."

The FCC adopted its label rules in November 2022. Providers with more than 100,000 subscribers had to comply from April 10, 2024, and all other providers from October 10, 2024. The aim is a standard format, so plans from different providers can be compared field by field.

## What is on the label?

According to the FCC's order, a label must:

| Label content | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| Price, including introductory rates | The monthly price, and if it is a promotion, how long it lasts and what you pay afterward |
| One-time and recurring fees | Charges on top of the base price |
| Data allowance | Whether usage above a monthly amount is limited or charged |
| Contract term, if any | How long you commit, and early termination fees if they apply |
| Typical download and upload speed | Speeds in megabits per second (Mbps) the provider says are typical for that plan |
| Typical latency | Delay in milliseconds (ms); lower is better, mostly noticeable in calls and games |
| Links to network management practices and the privacy policy | Where to read how the provider manages traffic and handles data |
| Language | English, plus any other language the provider uses to market the plan in the US |

Sources: FCC-26-48, paragraph 6, for the first six content items and the language rule; the FCC press release for the privacy policy link. Paragraph 6 describes the list as covering these items "among other things," so the full label template contains more than we list here.

## How do I compare two plans using labels?

1. **Use the price after the introductory period.** A promotional price only counts for the months it lasts. Add up the full contract period, not the first month.
2. **Add the fees.** Fees that vary by location are the part most likely to change the real monthly price.
3. **Check the term and the early termination fee.** A cheaper plan with a two-year commitment costs more to leave.
4. **Match speeds to your use, not to the biggest number.** Our [household speed guide]({{< relref "/guides/how-much-internet-speed-do-i-need" >}}) explains how much a family actually uses.
5. **Look at upload speed and latency if you work from home, make video calls or game.** See our [bufferbloat guide]({{< relref "/guides/fix-bufferbloat-gaming-lag" >}}) for why latency under load matters.
6. **Check that equipment fees are accounted for.** The documents we reviewed do not say whether equipment rental appears as a label fee, so confirm it with the provider. Our guide to [buying your own router vs. renting from the provider]({{< relref "/guides/buy-your-own-router-vs-isp-equipment" >}}) explains the trade-off.

### A worked example (hypothetical prices)

Two plans, both for 24 months, using the maximum fees each label states:

| | Plan A | Plan B |
|---|---|---|
| Base price, months 1-12 | $60 per month | $75 per month |
| Base price, months 13-24 | $80 per month | $75 per month |
| Maximum fees | $10 per month | $4 per month |
| Total months 1-12 | ($60 + $10) × 12 = $840 | ($75 + $4) × 12 = $948 |
| Total months 13-24 | ($80 + $10) × 12 = $1,080 | ($75 + $4) × 12 = $948 |
| Total over 24 months | $1,920 | $1,896 |

Plan A costs $9 less per month in year one ($70 vs. $79) and $11 more per month in year two ($90 vs. $79), which makes it $24 more expensive over two years. These figures are invented to show the method; real labels will differ, and actual fees may be below the stated maximum.

## What is the FCC changing in 2026?

On July 22, 2026, the FCC adopted Report and Order FCC 26-48, released July 23. It was published in the Federal Register on August 13, 2026. The FCC says the 2022 rules produced labels that were "sometimes-confusing" and increased compliance costs. The order keeps the labels but changes how they are shown. When the changes take effect, providers may:

- **Use a link or icon at the point of sale** instead of the full label. The link must go directly to that plan's label (or a page where the plan is identifiable without more navigation, address entry or search), appear close to the advertised plan, and be clearly identified as leading to the label.
- **Use a link or icon in the customer account portal.** The label reached must be for the customer's current plan.
- **Show location-based passthrough fees as an "up to" total** or as the exact total for a jurisdiction, instead of itemizing each fee. An "up to" total must also be split into a maximum for government-imposed fees and a maximum for fees attributable to non-government third parties, with the fee types listed and a link to a page describing them. The figure must not understate current fees, and the label must be revised if fees rise above it. Providers may still itemize.
- **Summarize the label by phone** instead of reading it verbatim. The order requires the summary to cover: monthly price including monthly fees (and any introductory rate and its duration), typical download and upload speeds, latency, data allowance, contract term and early termination fees.

The order also removes the reference to the Affordable Connectivity Program, a federal discount program that ended June 1, 2024, and eliminates two provider obligations: supplying label contents as a machine-readable spreadsheet and archiving labels for two years after a plan is no longer sold to new customers. It keeps the requirements that labels be accessible to people with disabilities and appear in the languages the provider markets in.

### When do the changes take effect?

This is the part to read carefully, because the dates differ by section.

- **Adopted:** July 22, 2026. **Published in the Federal Register:** August 13, 2026.
- **September 14, 2026:** the date the Federal Register notice gives as the effective date for the order, **except** for "instruction 3 (§ 8.1(a)), which is delayed indefinitely." That instruction contains the changes to the label requirements themselves. What took effect on September 14 were the definitions in 47 CFR 8.1(b), including a definition of "passthrough fee."
- **Still pending:** the FCC says it "will publish a document in the Federal Register announcing the effective date" for the delayed part. The notice says the order may contain new or revised information-collection requirements, which the Office of Management and Budget (OMB) must review under the Paperwork Reduction Act. As of October 12, 2026, we did not find that announcement.

In practice: until the FCC announces a date, the existing 2022 label rules remain the legal requirement, and a provider that already shows links or "up to" fees is acting ahead of them. We have not checked any individual provider's practices.

## What the label cannot tell you

- **It is not your bill.** The FCC's order says labels "may not specify every charge or discount associated with a consumer's current service plan, and thus may not serve as a comprehensive billing reference document."
- **"Typical" is the provider's figure.** In the rulemaking, one commenter argued that typical-speed disclosures are not comparable between providers, and asked the FCC to define "typical." The FCC declined, saying the current rule is sufficient. If speed matters, test your own connection, and see our guide to [Wi-Fi that is slow when the internet is fast]({{< relref "/guides/wifi-slow-but-internet-fast" >}}), since your router and Wi-Fi also limit what you get.
- **It describes a plan, not your address.** What a provider sells depends on where you live. Our [fiber vs. cable vs. 5G comparison]({{< relref "/guides/fiber-vs-cable-vs-5g-home-internet" >}}) covers how to check availability.

## Bottom line

Use the label to compare the whole cost over the contract, not the first month's price. The 2026 FCC order keeps the labels and will make them easier to reach through links, but as of October 12, 2026 its label changes are still waiting on an effective-date announcement. Check the date on any label you rely on, and confirm the all-in monthly price with the provider before you order.
