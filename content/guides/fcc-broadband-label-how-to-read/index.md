An FCC broadband label is a standardized fact box that every US internet provider must publish for each standalone plan it currently offers. Read six things on it before you sign: the monthly price and when any introductory rate ends, the itemized monthly fees, the typical speeds and latency, the data allowance, the contract term, and the early termination fee. This guide explains each, then covers what the Federal Communications Commission (FCC) changed in July 2026 and which of those changes are actually in force.

The FCC is the US federal agency that regulates broadband. Congress ordered the labels in a 2021 law, and the FCC wrote the rules in 2022. This guide covers the United States only.

## What is on a broadband label, and what should you check?

The FCC's July 22, 2026 news release says providers must have a label for each standalone plan they currently offer, and that the labels "must show introductory rates, data allowances, and broadband speeds, and must also include links to information about network management practices and privacy policies."

The FCC's rule text, as published in the Federal Register on August 13, 2026, names more of the fields. Some of that wording sits in the part of the rule that is delayed (explained below), but the fields themselves are ones the FCC's order describes as part of the label. The table below ties each field to a practical check.

| Label field | What to check | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Monthly price and introductory rate | Is the price a promotion? If so, how long does it last and what is the price afterward? | The rule treats an introductory rate and its duration as a label field. The price you pay after the promotion is the one that counts for the long run. |
| Monthly fees not included in the monthly price | Add every line to the monthly price. | The 2026 rule text says a label "shall itemize any monthly fees not included in the monthly price," with passthrough fees as the one exception once the delayed provision takes effect. |
| Passthrough fees | Are they itemized, or shown as one total? | See the section below. These are location-dependent charges. |
| Typical download and upload speeds | Does the upload speed fit how you work, such as video calls or backups? | Both are labeled "typical," not guaranteed. |
| Latency | Compare it if you game or make video calls. | Latency is the delay for data to travel, measured in milliseconds. |
| Data allowance | Is there a monthly cap? What happens above it? | Caps, and what happens above them, differ by plan. Read the plan details. |
| Contract term and early termination fee | Is there a minimum commitment? What does leaving early cost? | These are among the label fields the FCC requires a phone representative to summarize. |

Equipment rental is not named in the rule text we reviewed, but it is a common monthly fee. If you would rent a gateway or router from the provider, look for it among the itemized monthly fees. If it is missing, ask before you sign. Buying your own router can avoid a rental line; our [router versus mesh guide]({{< relref "/guides/wifi-router-vs-mesh" >}}) covers what to buy.

A label also does not replace a quote. In the July 2026 order the FCC acknowledges that labels "may not specify every charge or discount associated with a consumer's current service plan, and thus may not serve as a comprehensive billing reference document." Ask the provider for the expected first-month and ongoing monthly totals, including taxes, and keep a copy.

## Where do you find the label?

The FCC's April 10, 2024 launch announcement says providers must display the label at online and in-store points of sale, in close proximity to the advertisement for the plan it describes, and that labels are required for standalone home (fixed) internet plans and mobile broadband plans.

The 2026 rule text, once it takes effect, defines point of sale as the provider's website and any other channel through which it sells service, including provider-owned retail stores, third-party retail stores and telephone sales. It also covers "the time a consumer begins investigating and comparing broadband service offerings."

If your provider offers an online account portal, the order says it must give you easy access to the label for your current plan. That is useful when you want to check whether your bill matches the plan you signed up for.

## What is a passthrough fee?

The July 2026 order defines a passthrough fee as a monthly charge that:

1. is imposed by a government entity or third-party infrastructure owner rather than set by the provider itself;
2. represents costs the provider chooses to recover as a separate charge instead of building them into the base monthly price; and
3. varies by consumer location.

Because these fees vary by location, listing each one can mean a provider needs many different labels. The order lets providers show them in total instead. Once that provision takes effect, a provider could show either:

- **a maximum ("up to") total** that covers any location where the plan is sold, split into an "up to" amount for government-imposed costs and an "up to" amount for costs imposed by non-governmental third parties, along with the types of fees in each group; or
- **the exact total** for the area the label covers.

Providers may also keep itemizing fees one by one. The FCC says the displayed maximum or exact total "must not understate the current fees applicable to any consumer subscribing to that plan," and the provider must revise the label if fees rise above it. The FCC adds that some customers could see bills below an "up to" figure.

Here is a hypothetical example, not any real plan. A plan lists a $60 monthly price, a $10 monthly equipment fee and "up to $4.50" in passthrough fees. The most you would pay from those three lines is $60 + $10 + $4.50 = $74.50 per month. The rule text we reviewed does not say how taxes are shown, so ask whether taxes are extra.

## What did the FCC change in July 2026, and what is in force now?

The FCC voted on July 22, 2026 to adopt a Report and Order (FCC 26-48) that the Federal Register published as a final rule on August 13, 2026 (91 FR 52251). The FCC describes it as making labels "a more useful tool for consumers" and reducing compliance costs for providers. The FCC's July 1, 2026 fact sheet and the order list these changes:

- Phone sales representatives may present label information conversationally instead of reading it word for word. They must still orally summarize the monthly price (including monthly fees and any introductory rate and its duration), typical download and upload speeds, latency, data allowance, contract term, and early termination fees.
- Providers may show passthrough fees as a total, as described above.
- The reference to the Affordable Connectivity Program (ACP), a federal internet subsidy that ended June 1, 2024, is removed.
- A prominent link or icon in a customer's account portal can replace the full label there.
- A link or icon at the point of sale can replace the full label, if it connects directly to that plan's label and sits close to the advertised plan.
- Providers no longer have to supply label contents as a separate machine-readable spreadsheet file, and no longer have to archive labels for two years after a plan is discontinued.
- Labels must still be accessible to people with disabilities and shown in any language the provider uses to market the service.

**When do these take effect?** The Federal Register notice says: "Effective September 14, 2026, except for instruction 3 (§ 8.1(a)), which is delayed indefinitely." The notice adds that the FCC will publish a document announcing the effective date. As published, the instruction that takes effect on September 14 revises the definitions in section 8.1(b), including the definition of "passthrough fee." Instruction 3 carries the revised wording on label display, fee presentation, point-of-sale links and the telephone summary, and removes some provisions. The notice says the order may contain new information collection requirements that need review by the Office of Management and Budget under the Paperwork Reduction Act.

Our reading is that the display and fee changes listed above are delayed. Some law-firm and trade-press summaries describe only the passthrough-fee provision as delayed, while the rule text puts the point-of-sale, portal and telephone wording in the same delayed instruction. We rely on the Federal Register text. As of October 6, 2026, we found no Federal Register notice announcing an effective date for instruction 3. Until the FCC announces one, the label rules in place since 2024 remain the baseline, and practices can differ by provider. If a provider shows only a link or icon instead of a full label, you can still ask for the full label.

## How do labels fit into choosing a plan?

A label tells you what a provider says about one plan. It does not tell you which plans are available at your address, or how that provider performs in your home. For help choosing between fiber, cable and 5G home internet, see our [fiber versus cable versus 5G guide]({{< relref "/guides/fiber-vs-cable-vs-5g-home-internet" >}}). Once you have service, our [slow Wi-Fi test guide]({{< relref "/guides/how-to-fix-slow-wifi" >}}) shows how to check the speed you actually get.

One caution about typical speeds. In the July 2026 order, the FCC considered a commenter's request to keep examining how "typical" performance is defined, because the commenter said disclosures are not comparable between providers. The FCC concluded that "the current rule is sufficient." So treat the typical speed on a label as an indication, and compare providers with that in mind.

## Bottom line

Before signing for a home internet plan, find its label and read the monthly price, the date any promotional rate ends, every itemized monthly fee, the typical speeds and latency, the data allowance, and the cancellation terms. Then ask for the expected monthly total in writing, because the FCC says labels may not show every charge. The FCC's July 2026 order loosens how labels are shown and how fees are listed, but as published on August 13, 2026 those display and fee changes are delayed indefinitely, so check the [FCC's broadband label page](https://www.fcc.gov/broadbandlabels) for an announced effective date before assuming the new formats apply.
