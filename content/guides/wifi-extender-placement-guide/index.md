Put a Wi-Fi extender roughly halfway between your router and the room with weak Wi-Fi, at a spot where it still gets a strong signal from the router. That is the rule both Netgear and TP-Link give. In practice it usually means 25 to 40 feet from the router, at about the router's height, and closer than halfway if walls or cabinets are in the way. The extender's own signal light tells you whether you got it right.

This is a research-based guide built from Netgear and TP-Link's placement documentation. We have not tested extender placement ourselves.

## Why not put the extender in the dead zone?

An extender is a relay. It listens to your router's Wi-Fi and rebroadcasts it, so it can only pass on the signal it receives. Netgear says the extender "must be within the wireless range" of the router. In a room where your phone barely connects, the extender would barely connect too, and everything it serves would inherit that weak link.

The opposite mistake is putting it right next to the router. The link to the router is strong there, but the extender's coverage barely reaches past what the router already covers. Halfway is the compromise between those two.

## How far from the router should a Wi-Fi extender be?

| Placement factor | Netgear | TP-Link |
|---|---|---|
| Where | About halfway between the router and the device or area you want to cover, within the router's range | About halfway between the router and the dead zone, within the router's range |
| Distance | 25 to 40 feet, with line of sight if possible | 25 to 40 feet for external antennas, 20 to 35 feet for internal antennas, with few obstacles |
| Obstacles | Thick walls weaken the link; keep a clear path | Place it closer to the router if walls or other objects are in the way |
| Height | About the router's height, roughly four feet up | About the router's height, around four feet up; avoid the floor and corners |
| Antennas | Upright, pointed toward the router | Vertical; face the front of the extender toward the router |
| Keep away from | Microwaves, cordless phones, baby monitors | Microwaves, cordless phones, refrigerators, baby monitors, mirrors, large metal surfaces |

The distances assume an open path. Every wall, floor, cabinet and appliance between the router and the extender shortens them, which is why the real spot is often closer to the router than the tape-measure halfway point. If a whole floor or several rooms are weak, an extender is the wrong tool; see our [dead-zone guide](/guides/find-and-fix-wifi-dead-zones/) and our comparison of [access points, extenders and mesh](/guides/access-point-vs-extender-vs-mesh/).

## How do I find the right spot?

1. **Set it up using your extender's quick start guide.**
2. **Pick a spot about halfway to the weak area.** Use an outlet at roughly four feet if you can. TP-Link suggests a power strip to make height and direction easier to adjust.
3. **Check the signal light.** It shows how well the extender hears the router.
4. **Move it closer to the router if the light is red,** then check again.
5. **Test from the weak room.** Connect a phone or laptop to the extender and run a speed test where you actually need Wi-Fi.

## What do the extender lights mean?

| Brand | Light | What it means | What to do |
|---|---|---|---|
| Netgear | Router Link LED green | Best connection to the router | Leave it |
| Netgear | Router Link LED amber | Good connection | Leave it |
| Netgear | Router Link LED red | Poor connection | Move it closer to the router |
| TP-Link | Signal LED solid on | Connected to the router | Check its color or bars for strength |
| TP-Link | Signal LED red | Weak signal from the router | Move it closer to the router |

Netgear says the extender is "working as expected" when both its Router Link and Client Link LEDs are green or amber. Light styles vary by model, so check your extender's quick start guide for its exact colors.

## What if no spot works?

Run a cable. TP-Link suggests switching the extender to access point mode, placing it where you need coverage and connecting it to the router with Ethernet. Nothing is relayed over the air that way, so the speed loss from relaying disappears. If you can't run Ethernet, our [MoCA guide](/guides/what-is-moca-ethernet-over-coax/) covers using your coax wiring instead.

Netgear also warns against chaining extenders, one feeding another. It says that can make the connection sluggish. For a large house, a mesh system usually beats a chain of extenders; our [best mesh Wi-Fi for a large house](/guides/best-mesh-wifi-for-large-house/) guide covers the options, and our [best Wi-Fi extenders](/guides/best-wifi-extenders/) guide covers single-room picks.

## Bottom line

Halfway is the right starting point, as long as the extender still hears the router well. Start about 25 to 40 feet out and about four feet up, let the signal light decide, and move closer to the router whenever it shows red. If no wireless spot gives you a good light and good speeds in the weak room, wire an access point or switch to mesh.
