The best Wi-Fi extender for most homes is one that joins your router's own network family and has a gigabit Ethernet port, such as the TP-Link RE715X (Wi-Fi 6, EasyMesh-compatible) or the ASUS RP-BE58 (Wi-Fi 7, AiMesh). If you only need to rescue one room and spend as little as possible, the TP-Link RE315 cost $22.97 at B&H when we checked on October 21, 2026. If you have a Netgear router, the EAX17 ($99.99 at Netgear's US store the same day) is Netgear's own Wi-Fi 6 mesh extender. And if you can run a cable to where the extender goes, use access point mode: it skips the speed cost of relaying Wi-Fi over the air.

This is a research-based buying guide. We have not tested these extenders. Specs come from each manufacturer's US product page (or, for the RE315, a US retailer's spec listing), checked on October 21, 2026, and prices are the ones shown that day. Prices change often, so check before you buy.

## First, check that an extender is the right fix

An extender helps when one or two rooms are weak and the rest of the house is fine. It is the wrong tool when:

- **Several rooms or floors are weak.** Chaining extenders adds a wireless hop each time, so a mesh system is often the better fit; our [router vs. mesh guide]({{< relref "/guides/wifi-router-vs-mesh" >}}) helps you decide.
- **You can run a cable, or have coax or good wiring.** A wired link to a second access point usually beats a wireless relay. MoCA adapters (over coax) and powerline adapters (over electrical wiring) are other ways to get a wired-style link.
- **The problem isn't coverage.** If the room has strong signal but is still slow, start with our [dead zone guide]({{< relref "/guides/find-and-fix-wifi-dead-zones" >}}) to rule out other causes.

## Why extenders lose speed, and how to avoid it

A standard extender listens to your router and talks to your devices. TP-Link's own buying guide says extenders "may reduce available bandwidth" because "many models use the same radio to receive and rebroadcast the signal." TP-Link doesn't put a number on the loss, and it depends on the model and placement.

Three things limit it:

1. **An extra band.** TP-Link says Wi-Fi 7 extenders are "commonly tri-band," which can leave one band free to talk to the router, depending on the model.
2. **An Ethernet cable.** Most extenders here have an Ethernet port and an access point mode. Wire the extender to your router and it no longer relays over the air at all.
3. **Placement.** An extender can only pass on the signal it receives. Put it where it still hears the router well, which may be closer to the router than you expect. Our [extender placement guide]({{< relref "/guides/wifi-extender-placement-guide" >}}) explains how to find that spot.

A note on box ratings: names like AX3000 or BE11000 add up the maximum link rate of every band. TP-Link's RE655BE page says its figures are physical rates from the 802.11 standards, not guaranteed throughput. No single device will see that number.

### One weak room on a tight budget: TP-Link RE315

The RE315 is a plug-in Wi-Fi 5 (802.11ac) extender rated AC1200, which B&H lists as up to 1,167 Mbps across its two bands. It works with TP-Link OneMesh routers, so you can keep one network name with a compatible TP-Link router, and it has an access point mode. The catch is its Ethernet port: it runs at 10/100 Mbps, so it caps a wired device, or a wired access point link, at 100 Mbps. That's fine for a bedroom TV or a home office on a modest plan, not for a gigabit plan. B&H listed it at $22.97 (down from $29.97) on October 21, 2026. We didn't confirm the hardware version on sale, so check the box against TP-Link's spec page.

### Most homes: TP-Link RE715X

The RE715X is a dual-band Wi-Fi 6 extender rated AX3000: 2,402 Mbps on 5 GHz and 574 Mbps on 2.4 GHz. TP-Link's US page lists it as EasyMesh-compatible, with one gigabit Ethernet port and a built-in access point mode. EasyMesh is a Wi-Fi Alliance standard for mixing compatible devices in one mesh, so the RE715X suits EasyMesh-compatible routers, including TP-Link models that support EasyMesh. Check your router's spec sheet for EasyMesh support before you count on it. We didn't find a price we could verify today.

### Netgear router owners: Netgear EAX17

Netgear's page description says the EAX17 "creates a mesh WiFi 6 system with your existing router," with one Wi-Fi name and roaming between them; it doesn't say whether that requires a Netgear router or which features need one. Its spec table lists 600 Mbps on 2.4 GHz and 2,400 Mbps on 5 GHz (up to 3 Gbps combined, though one section of the same page lists different figures), one gigabit Ethernet port and WPA3. Netgear's store listed it at $99.99, down from $129.99, on October 21, 2026. The page doesn't mention EasyMesh or an access point mode, so don't assume either.

### ASUS router owners: ASUS RP-BE58

The RP-BE58 is a dual-band Wi-Fi 7 extender rated BE3600: 688 Mbps on 2.4 GHz and 2,882 Mbps on 5 GHz. It has no 6 GHz radio. ASUS says it supports AiMesh, so it can join a compatible ASUS router's AiMesh network, and it offers range extender, access point and media bridge modes, with one gigabit port. It was $99.99 at the ASUS US eShop on October 21, 2026. Its Wi-Fi 7 features only pay off on the link to your router if that router is also Wi-Fi 7; with an older ASUS router, the extender falls back to what both support.

### A fast Wi-Fi 7 network: TP-Link RE655BE

The US RE655BE is a tri-band Wi-Fi 7 extender rated BE11000: up to 5,764 Mbps on 6 GHz, 4,323 Mbps on 5 GHz and 688 Mbps on 2.4 GHz, with one 2.5 Gbps Ethernet port and both range extender and access point modes. TP-Link says it is compatible with EasyMesh but "has not obtained the Wi-Fi EasyMesh™ certification." It's aimed at Wi-Fi 7 routers and internet plans above 1 Gbps; on a slower setup, a Wi-Fi 6 model like the RE715X covers the same job. Check that the box says BE11000, the rating on TP-Link's US page. TP-Link's page sends buyers to Best Buy, and we couldn't verify a price today.

## A note on FCC rules and TP-Link

On March 23, 2026, the FCC added "routers produced in a foreign country" to its Covered List, except routers granted a Conditional Approval. The rule blocks FCC authorization of new models; models authorized before the change can still be sold, and devices you own are unaffected. The FCC documents we read don't say whether range extenders count as routers. Either way, the extenders above are existing models on sale today. If you're weighing a TP-Link product for other reasons, our report on the [state lawsuits against TP-Link]({{< relref "/news/tp-link-state-lawsuits-what-owners-should-know" >}}) covers what owners should know.

## How to choose in two minutes

1. **Look up your router's brand and mesh support.** If it supports TP-Link OneMesh, EasyMesh or ASUS AiMesh, buy an extender from the same family so you keep one network.
2. **Match the Wi-Fi generation to your router,** not above it, unless you're upgrading the router soon.
3. **Check the Ethernet port speed** against your internet plan if you'll ever wire it.
4. **Plan to wire it if you can.** Access point mode over Ethernet is the biggest speed upgrade any of these offer.
