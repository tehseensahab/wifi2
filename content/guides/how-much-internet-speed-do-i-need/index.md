Most US households need less speed than they are sold. A plan around 100 Mbps download and 20 Mbps upload handles several 4K streams, video calls and online games at the same time. Faster plans are worth it mainly when you download or upload very large files often, or when many people are online at once.

Speed here means megabits per second (Mbps), the unit on every internet plan. Download is data coming to you; upload is data you send. The way to size a plan is to add up what your household does at the same moment, then check the upload number separately.

## What does the FCC count as fast enough?

The Federal Communications Commission (FCC) uses 100 Mbps download and 20 Mbps upload as its benchmark for fixed home broadband. It adopted that figure on March 14, 2024, calling it a four-fold increase from the 25/3 Mbps benchmark set in 2015. The FCC's next report, released on August 14, 2026, kept the 100/20 Mbps benchmark.

The benchmark is a policy yardstick for measuring where broadband is available. It is not a rule about what you must buy, but it is a sensible reference point for a household.

These are the service providers' own recommendations, per stream or per call.

|---|---|---|---|

| Netflix, Full HD 1080p | 5 Mbps | | Netflix |
| Netflix, 4K | 15 Mbps | | Netflix |
| YouTube, 1080p | 5 Mbps | | YouTube |
| YouTube, 4K | 20 Mbps | | YouTube |
| Zoom group call, 720p | 1.8 Mbps | 2.6 Mbps | Zoom |
| Zoom group call, 1080p | 3.0 Mbps | 3.8 Mbps | Zoom |
| Cloud gaming, 1080p at 60 frames per second | 25 Mbps | | NVIDIA GeForce NOW |
| Cloud gaming, 4K at 120 frames per second | 45 Mbps | | NVIDIA GeForce NOW |

The FCC publishes a more general guide, last reviewed on July 18, 2022. It lists minimum download speeds for one activity at a time:

| Activity (FCC guide) | Minimum download |
|---|---|
| General browsing and email | 1 Mbps |
| Social media | 1 Mbps |
| Streaming HD video | 5 to 8 Mbps |
| Streaming 4K video | 25 Mbps |
| HD video teleconferencing | 6 Mbps |
| Online multiplayer gaming | 4 Mbps |
| Telecommuting | 5 to 25 Mbps |
| File downloading | 10 Mbps |

The FCC says of its own table: "These numbers are rough guidelines and are not based on surveys or experiments conducted by the FCC." Treat every number here as a floor for one activity, not a guarantee.

Two things stand out. Ordinary online gaming uses very little speed; the FCC lists 4 Mbps. Cloud gaming, where the game runs on a remote server and is streamed to you like video, is the exception. And video calls are the one common activity where upload matters as much as download.

## How do I work out what my household needs?

Add up the activities that happen at the same time on your busiest evening, then leave room on top. Here is a worked example using the FCC's figures, which are the more generous ones.

| At the same time | FCC minimum download |
|---|---|
| Two TVs streaming 4K (2 × 25) | 50 Mbps |
| One HD work video conference | 6 Mbps |
| One online multiplayer game | 4 Mbps |
| Two people browsing or on social media (2 × 1) | 2 Mbps |
| **Total** | **62 Mbps** |

That busy evening fits inside a 100 Mbps plan with more than a third to spare. Using Netflix's 15 Mbps figure for 4K, the total falls to 42 Mbps.

The spare room matters because devices also do things you don't see: phones back up photos, consoles and laptops download updates, and security cameras upload video. This is an estimate built from published minimums, not a measurement of any real home, so round up if you are unsure.

As a starting point, based on that arithmetic:

| Household | Reasonable plan | Why |
|---|---|---|
| One or two people, HD streaming, browsing, occasional calls | 50 to 100 Mbps | A 4K stream plus a call is about 31 Mbps on FCC figures |
| Three to five people, several streams, remote work or school | 100 to 300 Mbps | Covers the 62 Mbps example with room for updates and backups |
| Heavy use: frequent large downloads, cloud gaming on several screens, many cameras | 300 to 500 Mbps | Shortens big downloads while everyone else is online |
| Large uploads for work, such as video files or frequent backups | Choose by upload speed first | Download speed is rarely the limit here |

These ranges are our reading of the figures above. They are not an FCC recommendation. The FCC's own Household Broadband Guide still describes anything above 25 Mbps as "advanced service," but that page dates from July 18, 2022, before the benchmark rose to 100/20 Mbps.

## Why does upload speed matter?

Upload is what you send: your camera on a video call, files to cloud storage, footage from a security camera, a live stream.

Zoom lists 3.8 Mbps of upload for a 1080p group call. Two people on calls at once need about 7.6 Mbps. That fits within a 20 Mbps upload speed, but it would crowd a plan with 10 Mbps of upload once backups and cameras are added.

Plans are usually named for their download speed, so look for the upload figure too. Some connection types offer upload speeds far lower than their download speeds, while fiber plans often offer the same speed both ways. ## When is a faster plan worth paying for?

Mostly when you move large files. A stream uses a fixed amount of data per second, so extra speed does not improve it. A download uses everything available, so extra speed finishes it sooner.

Here is how long a 100 GB download, roughly a large modern game, takes at different speeds. This assumes the full plan speed for the whole download and nothing else using the connection, so real times will be longer.

| Plan speed | Time to download 100 GB |
|---|---|
| 100 Mbps | About 2 hours 13 minutes |
| 300 Mbps | About 44 minutes |
| 500 Mbps | About 27 minutes |
| 1 Gbps (1,000 Mbps) | About 13 minutes |

The math: 100 GB is 800,000 megabits (100 × 1,000 × 8). Divide by the plan speed in Mbps to get seconds.

If you download a game like that once a month, the difference between 300 Mbps and 1 Gbps is about half an hour a month. If you do it weekly, or work with large video files, a faster plan starts to earn its price.

## Does more speed fix lag or buffering?

Often not. Three things are commonly mistaken for a slow plan.

- **Latency.** Lag in games and delays on calls come from latency, the time data takes to make a round trip, measured in milliseconds (ms). NVIDIA, for example, requires less than 80 ms of latency to its data center for GeForce NOW, separately from its speed requirements. A faster plan does not lower latency.
- **Wi-Fi.** Your plan's speed is delivered to the modem. The wireless link to each device is separate and often slower. If a laptop plugged into the router gets full speed and your phone doesn't, the plan is fine. See [why Wi-Fi can be slow when your internet is fast]({{< relref "/guides/wifi-slow-but-internet-fast" >}}).
- **An upload queue.** One device uploading a large file can make everything else stutter. That is a router traffic problem, which our [bufferbloat guide]({{< relref "/guides/fix-bufferbloat-gaming-lag" >}}) explains.

Before upgrading, run a speed test on a computer connected to the router by Ethernet cable. If the result is close to what you pay for and things still feel slow, more speed is unlikely to help. Our [slow Wi-Fi checklist]({{< relref "/guides/how-to-fix-slow-wifi" >}}) walks through the next steps.

## How do I check what a plan really delivers?

Read the plan's broadband label. The FCC requires internet providers to display these labels at the point of sale, and says they must disclose "broadband prices, introductory rates, data allowances, and broadband speeds." Most providers had to comply by April 10, 2024, and providers with 100,000 or fewer subscriber lines by October 10, 2024.

On the label, check three things:

1. **The speeds listed, for both download and upload.** Compare them with your total from the worksheet above.
2. **The price after any introductory rate ends.** A cheaper plan at a faster speed is not cheaper if the rate rises later.
3. **Any data allowance.** A household that streams 4K daily can use a lot of data, and a cap changes the value of the plan.

## Bottom line

Add up what your household does at once, compare it with the plan's download and upload speeds, and leave some room. For most homes that points to a plan at or a little above 100 Mbps down and 20 Mbps up. Pay for more when large downloads or uploads are a regular part of your week. If things feel slow on a plan that already covers your total, look at Wi-Fi and latency before you look at a bigger plan.
