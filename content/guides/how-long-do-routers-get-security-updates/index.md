A router that no longer receives security updates still carries your traffic, but any flaw found after its end date stays unpatched. Manufacturers measure that end date differently: eero publishes a date per model, Netgear counts three years from a model's last sale, and ASUS keeps a list of models it has ended. This guide compares what each says, shows how to work out your own date, and explains what to do when it passes.

This is a research-based guide built on each company's support pages and an FBI public service announcement, all checked on October 13, 2026. We have not tested any firmware ourselves. Policies change, so confirm your model on the maker's page before you act.

## Short answer: it depends on the brand and on when the model was last sold

| Brand | How the end date is set | What the maker publishes |
|---|---|---|
| eero | At least five years from the date you bought the device new on eero.com or Amazon.com, or a listed date for the model, whichever is later | A table of dates by model, March 11, 2030 to October 31, 2031 |
| Netgear | "In general," three years after the model's last sale date | An End of Service page with a model search and a full list of ended products |
| ASUS | Per model, when it is marked End-of-Life | An End-of-Life product list; we found no stated number of years |
| TP-Link (US pages) | Per model, when it reaches End of Service | An advisory confirming that End of Service products no longer get active support, including security updates; we found no general support period on the US pages we checked |

"Security update" here means a software fix, delivered as firmware, that closes a flaw. "End of Service" (Netgear's and TP-Link's term) and "End of Life" (ASUS's term) both describe the point at which that stream of fixes stops. The vocabulary is not standard across brands.

## What each manufacturer says

### eero

eero's support page says it will support devices "with guaranteed software security updates until at least five years from when you bought them new on eero.com or Amazon.com (or until the date stated below if later)." If you bought somewhere else, the guarantee runs until at least the listed date. After that, eero says it "strive[s] to provide software security updates for as long as we can (subject to technical and other limitations)," and that this "is not a guarantee."

The page's table, which covers devices currently sold on eero.com and Amazon.com, lists these dates:

| Device | Security updates at least through |
|---|---|
| eero Pro, eero Beacon | March 11, 2030 |
| eero (2nd Generation) | April 28, 2030 |
| eero Pro 6 | July 14, 2030 |
| eero 6, eero 6 Extender, eero 6+, eero Pro 6E, eero PoE 6, eero PoE Gateway, eero Max 7, eero Outdoor 7, eero 7, eero Pro 7, eero PoE 7, eero Signal, eero Telephone Adapter | October 31, 2031 |

**A worked example.** Suppose you buy an eero 7 new on Amazon.com on October 13, 2026. Five years later is October 13, 2031. The table date for the eero 7 is October 31, 2031, which is later, so the guarantee runs through October 31, 2031. Now suppose you buy an eero Pro 6 new on the same day. Five years is October 13, 2031, but the table date is July 14, 2030, so the later date, October 13, 2031, applies. This calculation follows eero's stated rule; it is our arithmetic, not a figure eero publishes for these purchases.

### Netgear

Netgear's End of Service page says products that reach End of Service "no longer receive firmware updates, including important security updates." It says End of Service "in general" occurs three years after the last sale date, which it defines as the last date Netgear reasonably anticipates the product will be available to buy through authorized sellers and the date it removes the product from its own web store. It adds that the last sale date "may be limited to a particular SKU in an identified country or region." SKU means a specific product variant.

Two things follow from that wording. First, the window is counted from when sales stop, not from when you bought. A router bought years before its last sale date gets more than three years from purchase; one bought near the last sale date gets about three. Second, the date is set per model and region, so you need your exact model. Netgear's page offers a model search, a full list, and EOS alerts if you register your product.

Netgear's page also says that if you keep using a product after its End of Service date, "you accept the risk of using a product that is no longer supported" and that it recommends upgrading. It advises factory resetting an ended product, removing it from your MyNETGEAR account and recycling it, and turning off Remote Access first if you enabled it.

### ASUS

ASUS's support FAQ (last updated May 28, 2024) says an End-of-Life product is no longer manufactured, its certification will not be renewed, and "its firmware, utility, website, Marketing materials, QSG and manual will not be updated." It says ASUS maintains an End-of-Life product list, which ASUS describes as "instantly maintained," and that End-of-Life status does not change the hardware warranty. The list page, checked October 13, 2026, covers many router series, including older RT-N and RT-AC models.

We did not find a stated number of years of firmware support on these ASUS pages. That means a model that is not on the list is not thereby promised any particular future support. It only means ASUS has not marked it End-of-Life.

### TP-Link

On TP-Link's US support page, a security advisory dated November 2024 says that the routers it concerned "have reached their End of Service (EOS) and are no longer receiving active support, including security updates." Even so, TP-Link published patched firmware for three models, the Archer C7(EU) V2, TL-WR841N(MS) V9 and TL-WR841ND(MS) V9, and recommended upgrading to newer hardware.

That is a useful illustration that fixes for ended products are possible, but it was an exception tied to one vulnerability, and TP-Link still recommended upgrading. We did not find a general TP-Link support-period policy on the US pages we checked, so for a TP-Link router, check your model's support page and firmware download page for its latest firmware date. For news on TP-Link's wider situation, see our [report on the state lawsuits]({{< relref "/news/tp-link-state-lawsuits-what-owners-should-know" >}}).

## Why an ended router is a risk, and how big a risk

The FBI's May 7, 2025 announcement explains the mechanism. It says that when a device is end of life, the manufacturer "is not actively supporting the hardware, which also means they are no longer releasing software updates or security patches." It says routers dated 2010 or earlier likely no longer receive manufacturer updates, and that some end-of-life routers with remote administration turned on were compromised by a new variant of the TheMoon malware, which lets criminals "install proxies" on victims' routers to hide their activity. The FBI lists overheating, connectivity problems and unrecognized settings changes as common signs of infection.

Read this carefully. The FBI's example concerns very old routers with remote administration enabled, and it does not say that every router past its end date will be compromised. Our reading is that the risk grows over time as new flaws are published and stay unpatched, and that turning off features that expose the router to the internet, such as remote management, reduces it. That is analysis, not a finding from the FBI or the manufacturers.

## How to check your own router

1. **Find the exact model and hardware version.** It is on the label on the router. Netgear says a product with no version shown is version 1; ASUS says to confirm the hardware version on the product or box label.
2. **Look it up.** eero: the security updates table. Netgear: the End of Service model search. ASUS: the End-of-Life list. Other brands: the model's support page.
3. **Check the firmware's age.** If the maker's download page shows no new firmware for years, treat that as a warning sign. That is our rule of thumb, not a manufacturer statement.
4. **Note the date.** If your model is listed or the date is within a year or two, start planning a replacement.
5. **If it is an internet provider's gateway,** ask the provider, since policies on this page apply to the brands named. Our guide on [buying your own router versus using your provider's]({{< relref "/guides/buy-your-own-router-vs-isp-equipment" >}}) covers the trade-offs.

## What to do when support ends

- **Replace it if you can.** This is the FBI's advice, and Netgear's and TP-Link's. Compare current options in our [mesh vs. router guide]({{< relref "/guides/wifi-router-vs-mesh" >}}) and [Wi-Fi 6E vs. Wi-Fi 7]({{< relref "/guides/wifi-6e-vs-wifi-7" >}}).
- **Until then,** install any available firmware (see our [firmware update how-to]({{< relref "/guides/how-to-update-router-firmware" >}})), turn off remote management, and use a long, unique admin password. These are the FBI's recommendations. Our [router hardening checklist]({{< relref "/guides/router-hardening-checklist" >}}) walks through them.
- **Before you recycle it,** do a factory reset, as Netgear advises, and remove it from any manufacturer account. eero says select devices may qualify for the Amazon Router Trade-In program.
- **When buying new,** check whether the model is still on sale. Under Netgear's rule, a model near its last sale date has a shorter remaining window than one just released. That is our inference from Netgear's wording, and the same logic does not apply to eero's purchase-date rule.

## Bottom line

There is no single answer to how long a router is supported. eero tells you a date, Netgear tells you a rule tied to the last sale date, and ASUS and TP-Link tell you when a model has already ended. Look up your exact model today, write down its date, and treat that date as your replacement deadline. A router past it does not fail, but it stops getting fixes.
