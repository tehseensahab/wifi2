The best router for an apartment is usually a single Wi-Fi 7 router, not a mesh kit. If you want Wi-Fi 7 in its entry-level BE3600 class, pick a dual-band model such as the **TP-Link Archer BE230** or **ASUS RT-BE58U**. If you want the simplest setup and a smart-home radio, pick the **eero 7**. If you live in a crowded building and your phones and laptops support 6 GHz, a tri-band router such as the **NETGEAR Nighthawk RS300** adds a band your neighbors' older routers can't use.

We haven't tested these routers. The picks are based on manufacturer specifications checked on October 28, 2026. The only price we could confirm at a retailer today was eero's own store, so other prices are left out.

## Quick picks

|---|---|---|

| Same class, full web interface, mesh-ready | ASUS RT-BE58U | BE3600 dual-band; 1 GB RAM; works as an AiMesh router or node |

| Crowded building, newer phones and laptops | NETGEAR Nighthawk RS300 | Tri-band with 6 GHz; one 2.5 Gbps internet port and two 2.5 Gbps LAN ports |

## What's different about apartment Wi-Fi

An apartment changes the problem. You have less space to cover and more neighbors' networks competing for the same airwaves.

**Coverage is rarely the issue.** Manufacturers rate single units well above the size of most apartments: eero lists the eero 7 at "up to 2,000 sq ft per device," and NETGEAR lists the RS300 at up to 2,500 sq ft. These are manufacturer claims. Concrete walls, a long hallway or a router stuck in a closet at one end of the unit will cut into them. Put the router near the middle of the apartment, in the open, before you think about adding mesh.

**Interference is the bigger issue.** In a building, your router shares channels with every network around you. The 2.4 GHz band reaches farthest but is the slowest and most crowded, as our [2.4 GHz vs. 5 GHz vs. 6 GHz guide]({{< relref "/guides/2-4-ghz-vs-5-ghz-vs-6-ghz-wifi" >}}) explains. The 6 GHz band is the least crowded, but only Wi-Fi 6E and newer devices can use it, and it reaches the least of the three bands. NETGEAR's own footnote for the RS300 says: "Clients must support 6GHz band." In an apartment, where you need less reach, the shorter range costs you less.

**Which band your devices use decides what you need.** All three dual-band picks here (BE230, RT-BE58U and eero 7) have only 2.4 GHz and 5 GHz radios. If your phones and laptops are older than Wi-Fi 6E, the 6 GHz band gives them nothing. See our [Wi-Fi 6E vs. Wi-Fi 7 guide]({{< relref "/guides/wifi-6e-vs-wifi-7" >}}) for which devices support what.

## The picks in detail

### TP-Link Archer BE230: an entry-level Wi-Fi 7 pick

TP-Link sells the Archer BE230 as a "BE3600 Dual-Band Wi-Fi 7 Router." It lists "2882 Mbps" on 5 GHz and "688 Mbps" on 2.4 GHz, which add up to about 3.6 Gbps, the figure on the box. TP-Link's own footnote calls those "physical rates derived from IEEE Standard 802.11 specifications" and says actual throughput "not guaranteed." Real speeds will be lower.

For ports, it has "1× 2.5 Gbps WAN," "1× 2.5 Gbps LAN" and "3× 1 Gbps LAN," plus a USB 3.0 port. The 2.5 Gbps WAN port only matters for internet speed if your plan is faster than about 1 Gbps; our guide to [multi-gig routers]({{< relref "/guides/do-you-need-a-multi-gig-router" >}}) covers the math. TP-Link says it is "Compatible with EasyMesh," with a caveat that mixing brands may fail, and that its HomeShield security has a "Free Basic Plan" while "Fees apply for the Pro Plan."

Before you buy a TP-Link router, know that five states have sued the company, alleging it misled consumers about router security and its ties to China. TP-Link denies the claims, no court has ruled and owning one remains legal; our [TP-Link lawsuit explainer]({{< relref "/news/tp-link-state-lawsuits-what-owners-should-know" >}}) has the details. The specs above come from TP-Link's global product page, which doesn't state the US hardware version.

### ASUS RT-BE58U: the same class with more control

The RT-BE58U sits in the same BE3600 class: ASUS lists "688+2882 Mbps," split as "up to 688 Mbps" on 2.4 GHz and "up to 2882 Mbps" on 5 GHz. Its operating bands are 2.4 GHz and 5 GHz only.

The ports are laid out a little differently: "1x 2.5G for WAN/LAN," "1x 1G for WAN/LAN" and "3 x 1G LAN," plus a USB 3.2 Gen 1 port. ASUS lists a "2.0 GHz quad-core processor" and "1 GB RAM." The bigger difference is growth: ASUS lists it as both a "Primary AiMesh Router" and an "AiMesh Node," so if you later move to a larger place, you can pair it with another ASUS router instead of starting over.

Pick it over the BE230 if you want ASUS's web interface and settings, or a mesh path inside one brand. We used ASUS's Canadian spec page; ASUS's US listing didn't come back in our searches.

### eero 7: the easiest to live with

The eero 7 is a mesh unit that works fine on its own. eero's comparison page lists it as "Dual-band" (2.4 GHz and 5 GHz, each 2x2), with wireless speeds "Up to 1.8 Gbps" and "Two 2.5 GbE ports." eero's store says it "supports 120+ devices" and supports Thread and Zigbee and works as a Matter controller, so it may replace a separate hub for compatible devices.

On October 28, 2026, eero.com listed one eero 7 at $169.99 and a two-pack at $284.99. Setup and settings live in eero's app; there's no full web interface like ASUS's, which suits people who want few settings to manage. If you're weighing eero's lineup, our [eero 7 vs. Pro 7 vs. Max 7 comparison]({{< relref "/guides/eero-7-vs-eero-pro-7-vs-eero-max-7" >}}) covers the step-ups.

### NETGEAR Nighthawk RS300: for crowded buildings and newer devices

The RS300 is the one pick here with a 6 GHz radio. NETGEAR lists it as tri-band (2.4 GHz, 5 GHz and 6 GHz) with "BE9300 WiFi 7 Performance," coverage up to 2,500 sq ft and up to 100 concurrent devices. It has one 2.5 Gbps internet port, two 2.5 Gbps LAN ports, two gigabit LAN ports and a USB 3.0 port. NETGEAR's footnote says the speed rating is a "Maximum wireless signal rate derived from IEEE 802.11be specifications."

Pick it if you live in a dense building, your 5 GHz connection struggles at busy times, and your main devices support Wi-Fi 6E or Wi-Fi 7. If they don't, a dual-band pick does the same job for less. NETGEAR's page also mentions an Armor security subscription with a trial; you don't need it for the router to work.

## Before you buy

- **Check your devices first.** If nothing you own supports 6 GHz, skip the tri-band price premium.
- **Check your plan.** A 2.5 Gbps internet port only helps with a plan faster than 1 Gbps and a modem or ONT with a matching port.
- **Check the building.** If your internet is provided by the building, ask whether you can add your own router.
- **Plan for updates.** Whichever brand you choose, turn on automatic firmware updates.
