A good upload speed for most US households is 20 Mbps. That is the upload half of the Federal Communications Commission's 100/20 Mbps benchmark for fixed home broadband, and it is several times what a video call needs. Upload speeds well above 20 Mbps are worth paying for when someone in the home streams live video, backs up large files to the cloud or sends big files for work. If your home mostly streams and browses, upload is rarely the number that holds you back.

Upload is the speed at which your devices send data out: your camera on a video call, photos syncing to the cloud, a game streamed to Twitch or YouTube, footage from a security camera. Speeds here are in megabits per second (Mbps). This guide is based on the FCC's reports and on each service's and provider's own published figures; we have not measured any connection ourselves.

## What is a good upload speed?

**20 Mbps is a good baseline; 50 Mbps or more gives a busy household headroom.** The FCC raised its fixed broadband benchmark "from 25/3 Mbps to 100/20 Mbps" in its 2024 Section 706 Report, released March 18, 2024, which defines adequate home broadband as "download speeds of at least 100 Mbps and upload speeds of at least 20 Mbps." The FCC's 2026 Section 706 Report (FCC 26-55), released August 14, 2026, still measured availability at 100/20 Mbps; the Commission's news release says "96.9% of Americans have access to a fixed terrestrial broadband service at 100/20 Mbps."

The 2026 report also dropped the longer-term goal of 1,000 Mbps down and 500 Mbps up that the 2024 report had set. Commissioner Anna Gomez's statement says the report "abolishes without replacement the future benchmark of 1,000/500 Mbps." So 20 Mbps up remains the federal yardstick, not a ceiling on what you can buy.

| Upload speed | What it handles comfortably | Who it suits |
|---|---|---|
| Under 10 Mbps | One or two video calls; photo backups run slowly | Light users; below the FCC benchmark |
| 10 to 20 Mbps | Several video calls at once | Home offices with regular video calls |
| 20 Mbps (FCC benchmark) | Calls plus cloud backups; one 1080p30 live stream at YouTube's recommended 14 Mbps only if little else is uploading | Typical households |
| 50 to 100 Mbps | A recommended-quality 1080p live stream alongside calls and backups | Streamers, home offices that send large files |
| 200 Mbps and up | Large uploads finish quickly | Video editors, heavy cloud backup, home servers |

The bands above are our reading of the per-service figures in the next section, not an official scale.

## How much upload speed do video calls, streaming and gaming need?

Each figure below is from the service's own requirements page and is per call, stream or session. Add them up for everything that runs at the same time.

| Activity | Upload needed | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Microsoft Teams one-to-one video call | 150 kbps minimum; 1.5 Mbps recommended; 4 Mbps best | Microsoft Learn |
| Microsoft Teams video meeting | 150 kbps minimum; 2.5 Mbps recommended; 4 Mbps best | Microsoft Learn |
| Google Meet video meeting | About 1 Mbps outbound per participant on average (large-organization planning figure); up to 1.7 Mbps at 720p and 3.6 Mbps at 1080p (small-organization table) | Google Workspace |
| YouTube Live, 720p (30 or 60 fps) | 3 Mbps minimum; 8 Mbps recommended | YouTube Help |
| YouTube Live, 1080p at 30 fps | 5 Mbps minimum; 14 Mbps recommended | YouTube Help |
| YouTube Live, 1080p at 60 fps | 6 Mbps minimum; 17 Mbps recommended | YouTube Help |
| YouTube Live, 4K at 60 fps | 14 Mbps minimum; 50 Mbps recommended | YouTube Help |
| PS Remote Play (PlayStation) | At least 5 Mbps up and down; 15 Mbps for best performance | PlayStation |

Microsoft lists its Teams figures in kilobits per second (1,000 kbps = 1 Mbps) and says Teams "can deliver HD video quality in under 1.5 Mbps." The YouTube figures are the H.264 bitrate settings for the encoder, and YouTube suggests "running a speed test to test your upload bitrate" before going live. Twitch publishes its own guidelines; we did not check them for this guide.

Video calls are where upload matters as much as download, but even three people on Teams meetings at Microsoft's recommended 2.5 Mbps use 7.5 Mbps. Live streaming is different: one 1080p stream at YouTube's recommended 14 to 17 Mbps uses most of a 20 Mbps upload by itself.

### A worked example

Two people in Teams meetings (2 × 2.5 Mbps = 5 Mbps) while a third streams to YouTube at 1080p and 30 fps (14 Mbps recommended) adds up to 19 Mbps. That fits under 20 Mbps on paper, with almost nothing left for phones backing up photos or a security camera. This is arithmetic from the published figures, not a measurement; real usage varies with the app, the picture and how many people are on screen, so leave headroom.

## How long does a large upload take?

Plan speed decides how long big uploads take. A 10 GB video file is about 80,000 megabits (10 × 8 × 1,000, using 1 GB = 1,000 MB). At a steady 20 Mbps that is 4,000 seconds, about 67 minutes. At 200 Mbps it is 400 seconds, under 7 minutes. Real uploads run slower than the plan speed because of Wi-Fi, network overhead and the server at the other end, so treat these as best cases.

If you upload files that size every week, a faster upload plan saves real time. If you upload them twice a year, it rarely matters.

## What upload speed do different types of internet offer?

In our reading of provider documents, upload speed varies widely between connection types. The figures below are each provider's own statements.

| Connection | Provider statement on upload | Source |
|---|---|---|
| 5G home internet | T-Mobile: "Typically between 12 – 55 Mbps" on its 5G Amplified, All-In and Rely plans; 11 to 30 Mbps on 5G Lite and capped plans (3 to 16 Mbps on 4G Lite and capped plans) | T-Mobile network performance page |
| Cable | Xfinity: new upload speeds in its upgraded ("enhanced") markets range "from 75–200 Mbps"; its X-Class plans, available in limited areas, offer "equally fast upload and download speeds above 200 Mbps" | Xfinity support |
| Fiber | Verizon: Fios offers "symmetrical upload and download speeds (on most plans)" | Verizon |

T-Mobile describes its ranges as projections "based on roughly the 25th and 75th percentiles of network tests," so a quarter of tests come in below the low number. On Xfinity, the faster upload depends on your equipment: the company says only "limited retail modems, eMTAs or gateways" support the upgraded speeds and that "customer-owned modems are not currently compatible with X-Class speeds." ## How do I test my upload speed?

1. **Test on a wired connection if you can.** Plug a laptop into the router with an Ethernet cable. A Wi-Fi test measures your Wi-Fi as well as your internet plan, and Wi-Fi is often the slower link.
2. **Pause other uploads.** Stop cloud backups, camera uploads and game streams while you test.
3. **Run two or three tests at different times of day** and note the upload figure each time.
4. **Compare the result with your plan's stated upload speed.** Plans are usually named after their download speed, so look up the upload figure on your provider's plan details.

If your wired result is well below what your plan promises, our guide to [why upload speed is slow](/guides/why-is-my-upload-speed-slow/) walks through the causes and fixes. If calls stutter only while someone else is uploading, that is often a queueing problem in the router, covered in our [bufferbloat guide](/guides/fix-bufferbloat-gaming-lag/).

## Who doesn't need a faster upload speed?

Households that mostly stream video, browse and game online. Streaming and browsing are download-heavy, and the services in the table above show that video calls use a few megabits per second of upload each. If nobody streams live, backs up large files or works with big media files, put your money into download speed and Wi-Fi coverage instead.
