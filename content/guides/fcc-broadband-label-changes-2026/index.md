The FCC changed its internet "nutrition label" rules in 2026, but the part most readers care about is not in force yet. The FCC adopted the changes on July 22, 2026. The Federal Register notice published on August 13, 2026 lists an effective date of September 14, 2026, with one large exception: the amendments to 47 CFR 8.1(a), which cover how labels are shown and what they must itemize, are "delayed indefinitely." Until the FCC announces a date for those, the 2022 label rules still apply to providers.

This is a regulatory explainer. It is based on the FCC's order and the Federal Register notice, and it applies to providers of home and mobile internet service in the United States. It isn't legal advice, and we haven't tested any provider's labels.

## What is a broadband label?

A broadband consumer label is a standard disclosure that providers of internet service must show for each stand-alone plan they sell. Congress required the labels in the Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act of 2021, and the FCC adopted rules to carry that out in November 2022.

According to the FCC's order, under those 2022 rules a label must:

1. Show prices, including introductory rates.
2. Show one-time and recurring fees and data allowances.
3. Give the contract length, if there is one.
4. Disclose typical upload and download speed and typical latency (the time data takes to make a round trip, measured in milliseconds).
5. Link to the provider's network management practices, among other things.
6. Appear in English and in the other languages in which the provider markets its service in the United States.

For most of these rules, providers with more than 100,000 subscriber lines had to comply by April 10, 2024, and other providers by October 10, 2024. The label rule covers mass-market retail plans. It doesn't apply to enterprise or special-access services.

## What did the FCC change on July 22, 2026?

The FCC adopted a Report and Order, numbered FCC 26-48, and released it on July 23, 2026. It says the changes make labels clearer and cut provider costs. Here is what it does, in the order's own terms:

| Change | What it means | In effect today? |
|---|---|---|
| Phone sales | Representatives may summarize the label aloud instead of reading it word for word. They must cover monthly price including monthly fees, introductory rate and its duration, typical download and upload speeds, latency, data allowance, contract term, and early termination fees where they apply. | No (section 8.1(a), delayed) |
| Passthrough fees | Providers may show these as an exact monthly total or a maximum ("up to") monthly total instead of itemizing each one. | No (section 8.1(a), delayed) |
| Point of sale and account portals | A hyperlink or icon placed near the advertised plan, which leads directly to that plan's label, can replace showing the full label. | No (section 8.1(a), delayed) |
| Machine-readable data and archiving | The requirements to publish label data in a machine-readable format and to keep labels for two years after a plan is no longer sold to new customers are removed. | No (section 8.1(a), delayed) |
| Affordable Connectivity Program | The requirement to show information about this ended program is removed. | No (section 8.1(a), delayed) |
| Definitions | New wording for "broadband internet access service" and "passthrough fee". | Yes, since September 14, 2026 |

The FCC left in place the core of the label: pricing, introductory rates, data allowances and performance metrics. The order also keeps the requirement that labels be accessible to people with disabilities and shown in the languages used to market the service.

## Why is only part of the order in effect?

This is the point most easily misread. Some coverage says the label changes "take effect September 14." The Federal Register notice is more specific. Its dates line reads: "Effective September 14, 2026, except for instruction 3 (Sec. 8.1(a)), which is delayed indefinitely. The Commission will publish a document in the Federal Register announcing the effective date."

The order explains the reason. The changes to section 8.1(a) will not take effect until the Office of Management and Budget (OMB) finishes reviewing any information-collection requirements that the FCC's Consumer and Governmental Affairs Bureau determines the Paperwork Reduction Act requires. The FCC will announce the date in a later public notice.

So, as of October 9, 2026:

- **In force:** the new definitions of broadband internet access service and "passthrough fee" in section 8.1(b).
- **Not yet in force:** every display and content change in the table above.

We searched for an FCC public notice or Federal Register document setting the 8.1(a) date and found none as of October 9, 2026. We can't rule out that one has been issued, so check the FCC's page for docket CG 22-2 before relying on this.

## What is a "passthrough fee," and what changes about fees?

Fees are where the order makes the biggest change to what a label can tell you. The new definition in section 8.1(b)(2), which is in effect, says a passthrough fee is a monthly charge that:

1. is imposed by a government entity or a third-party infrastructure owner rather than set by the provider itself;
2. the provider chooses to recover as a separate charge instead of including it in the base monthly price; and
3. varies by customer location.

The order gives state and local right-of-way fees and pole attachment fees charged by third-party pole owners as examples.

Once section 8.1(a) takes effect, a label must still itemize any monthly fee that is not part of the monthly price. The exception is passthrough fees, which may be shown together as either the exact monthly total or the maximum monthly total for the area the label covers. A provider using the "up to" option must also split that maximum into one figure for fees recovering government-imposed costs and one for fees recovering costs imposed by non-government infrastructure owners, and name the types of fees in each.

Consumer groups, including AARP and the Utility Reform Network, argued in the record that this could make it harder to understand what a customer will pay. The FCC rejected those arguments, writing that it expected most providers to show an "up to" amount or an exact total, and that accuracy requirements let consumers compare total recurring amounts across providers. Whether that holds in practice is untested; none of this applies yet.

## How to use a label today

Until the delayed rules take effect, the 2022 requirements apply. These are practical steps based on what the label must contain, not a test of any provider:

1. **Find the label for the exact plan.** The label is plan-specific. A provider may show different labels for different speeds, and different fees for different addresses.
2. **Compare the monthly price including fees.** A low advertised price can exclude monthly fees. Read the fee lines, not only the headline price.
3. **Check whether the price is introductory.** The label must say whether the price is an introductory rate and what you pay afterward. Compare the price after the intro period ends, using the same time frame for each provider.
4. **Note the contract term and early termination fee,** if any.
5. **Read typical speeds and latency as the provider's figures.** The label gives "typical" speeds. They are the provider's own disclosure, not an independent measurement. For how much speed you need, see our [internet speed guide]({{< relref "/guides/how-much-internet-speed-do-i-need" >}}).
6. **Compare technologies like for like.** Fiber, cable and 5G home internet differ in more than price; see [fiber vs. cable vs. 5G home internet]({{< relref "/guides/fiber-vs-cable-vs-5g-home-internet" >}}).
7. **If you are buying by phone,** ask the representative for the monthly price including all monthly fees, any introductory rate and how long it lasts, the contract term, and early termination fees. The FCC's rewritten phone rule lists these fields, though it isn't in force yet, so asking is a good habit rather than a right we can promise.

## What to watch next

Two things would change this picture: an FCC public notice announcing when section 8.1(a) takes effect, and the label formats providers adopt once links, icons and "up to" fee totals are allowed. We will update this article when the date is announced.

## Bottom line

The FCC has adopted looser label display rules, but the only part in force is a pair of definitions. For now, providers still owe you the 2022-style label for each plan, and the fee line and the post-introductory price are the figures most worth reading. If you are comparing plans in the coming months, check whether a provider has started using links or "up to" fee totals, and treat those as signs that the delayed rules have taken effect.
