Start with a wired speed test: plug a laptop into your router with an Ethernet cable and run a speed test. If that result is far below your plan, the problem is your modem, plan or provider. If it's fine and Wi-Fi is slow, the problem is in the wireless path, and the steps below will find it without spending money.

The order matters. Most "slow Wi-Fi" advice jumps straight to buying hardware. This sequence tells you whether you need any.

## How do I find out where the slowdown is?

### 1. Run a wired test

Connect a laptop to the router with an Ethernet cable. The FCC says a direct Ethernet connection gives the highest speeds and avoids Wi-Fi congestion, which makes it the best baseline.

For a clean result, Verizon's speed-test guidance suggests disconnecting any VPN and pausing video or music streaming during the test. Run it two or three times, since a single result can be off.

Compare the number to your plan. If it's close, your internet service is working. If it's well below, restart the modem (step 5), check whether your provider has an outage, and then contact them.

### 2. Test Wi-Fi next to the router, then in the problem room

Run the same test on Wi-Fi standing near the router, then in the room where it's slow. The gap tells you what you're dealing with:

### 3. Check whether it's one device or all of them

If every device slows down at the same time, the problem is upstream of any one device: the router, modem or provider. If only one phone or laptop is affected, start with that device.

### 4. Check how many things are using the connection

Cloud backups, game downloads, 4K streams and security cameras all share your connection. The FCC notes that when multiple wireless devices use the same Wi-Fi network, performance can suffer. Pause big downloads and test again.

### 5. Restart in the right order

Unplug the router and the modem. Plug the modem in first, wait for its lights to settle, then plug the router back in. Microsoft's support guidance notes that the lights tell you when each device is ready. If the modem's lights stay on after you unplug it, it may have a backup battery you need to remove.

A restart is a diagnostic, not a cure. If you need one every few days, something else is wrong, and the steps below are where to look.

### 6. Move the router

The FCC recommends putting the router in a central location. Get it out of cabinets, off the floor and away from large metal objects and thick walls. A router in a corner of the house spends half its signal on the yard.

### 7. Check which Wi-Fi band you're using

The FCC says 2.4 GHz is also used by many household devices, while 5 GHz offers faster speeds and may be less crowded by neighboring networks. The trade-off is that 5 GHz doesn't reach as far through walls.

Apple recommends giving every band the same network name so devices can choose, and notes that if you split them into separate names, devices may not connect reliably. If a device keeps landing on 2.4 GHz, check its Wi-Fi details (see our guide on Wi-Fi that's slow when the internet is fast).

### 8. Leave the channel on automatic, then check neighbors

Apple recommends letting the router pick the Wi-Fi channel automatically, since it will select the best one for you. The FCC notes that if your router's Wi-Fi settings show many other networks nearby, your performance could be affected by them. In a crowded apartment building, that's often the cause of evening slowdowns.

### 9. Update the router's firmware

Apple recommends setting routers and access points to install updates automatically. Updates can fix stability and performance bugs and close security holes. If your router is older and no longer gets updates, that's a real reason to consider replacing it.

### 10. Wire the devices that stay put

A TV, game console or desktop doesn't need Wi-Fi. Running Ethernet to it frees up wireless capacity for phones and laptops. If you can't run cable, the FCC says adapters are another route.

## When should I replace my router or call my provider?

Call your provider when the wired test is slow, when the modem's lights show a problem, or when many devices are slow at once and a restart doesn't help.

Consider new equipment only when your wired speed is good, the router is well placed and updated, and Wi-Fi is still weak in parts of the house. At that point, the question is coverage, and [our router-versus-mesh guide]({{< relref "/guides/wifi-router-vs-mesh" >}}) covers it. Also consider replacement if the router no longer receives firmware updates.

## What this means for you

Say you pay for 500 Mbps. A wired laptop tests at 480 Mbps, but your phone in the upstairs bedroom gets 40 Mbps. Your internet is fine. The phone is a few walls and a floor away from the router, possibly stuck on 2.4 GHz. Moving the router or adding a node halfway is the fix. A new, faster plan would change nothing.

## Bottom line

Test with a cable first, then work from the cheapest fixes to the most expensive: restart, move, check the band, update, wire what you can. Most slow Wi-Fi gets better before you reach the buy-new-gear step.
