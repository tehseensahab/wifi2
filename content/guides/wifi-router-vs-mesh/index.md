You need mesh if your router can't cover the whole house even after you've put it in a central spot. In a smaller one-story or open-plan home, a single router is cheaper, simpler and usually enough. Mesh earns its price in multi-story houses, big or oddly shaped homes, and homes with thick walls.

The catch is that many people buy mesh before testing whether a cheaper fix works. This guide helps you decide which one you need, and what to try first.

## What is the actual difference between a router and mesh?

A router is one box that broadcasts Wi-Fi from one spot. Signal fades with distance and with every wall and floor in the way.

A mesh system is a main unit plus one or more nodes that act as one network. Your phone connects to the closest one, and Tom's Guide notes the system hands your connection off automatically as you move around the house. There's one network name and no manual switching.

A Wi-Fi extender is a third option. It picks up your router's signal and rebroadcasts it. Tom's Guide says most extenders create a separate network you have to switch to, and they're less capable than mesh satellites. One reviewer of ASUS's ZenWiFi BT10 noted that old-style repeaters suffered a severe drop in bandwidth because they used the same radio to talk to the router and to your devices. Mesh systems with a dedicated link, or an Ethernet cable between units, avoid that.

## How do I tell which one my house needs?

Start with a test, not a purchase. Run a speed test next to the router, then in the room with the worst signal. The FCC suggests checking your speed first and notes that Wi-Fi performance depends on where the router sits and how many devices share it.

Then use these rough guides:

- **Small or medium, one floor, fairly open:** a single router, placed near the middle.
- **About 3,000 sq ft or more:** Tom's Guide says a regular router won't cut it at that size. For comparison, TP-Link rates a single Deco BE63 unit at 2,500 sq ft. These are rough guides, not rules.
- **Two or more floors, or an odd layout:** mesh is likely worth it. Tom's Guide says multi-story and oddly laid-out homes don't match the coverage pattern a standalone router delivers.
- **One stubborn dead spot and nothing else:** try cheaper fixes first, below.

Construction matters too. Brick, concrete and metal block Wi-Fi far more than drywall does, so a 2,000 sq ft home with masonry walls can need mesh while a 3,000 sq ft open house might not.

## How far does Wi-Fi really reach?

Tom's Guide tested several mesh kits in a multi-story home with brick walls. Its September 7, 2026 roundup shows how steeply speed falls with distance. At 15 feet, the kits it tested ranged from about 1.4 to 1.9 Gbps. At 90 feet, they ranged from 20.8 Mbps (eero Pro 7) to 150.1 Mbps (TP-Link Deco BE23). That's one home and one test laptop, so your numbers will differ, but the pattern is what matters: the far rooms are where a single unit struggles.

## What should I try before buying mesh?

These cost nothing or very little, and any of them can fix a problem that looks like a coverage problem.

1. **Move the router.** The FCC recommends a central location. Get it out of cabinets and away from large metal objects and thick walls.
2. **Check the band.** The FCC notes that 2.4 GHz is shared with many household devices, while the 5 GHz band is faster and may be less crowded by neighbors' networks.
3. **Wire what stays put.** The FCC says a direct Ethernet connection between your router and a device gives the highest speeds and avoids Wi-Fi congestion. A TV, console or desktop doesn't need Wi-Fi.
4. **Update the router.** Apple recommends setting routers to install firmware updates automatically.
5. **Look at the router itself.** If it's an older combination modem-router from your internet provider, a newer router may help, but check with your provider about how to set it up.

If those don't help, then compare options.

On cost, Tom's Guide says mesh systems range from about $80 to $1,000 or more, and individual extra units often sell for $100 to $200. You can also start with two units and add a third later.

## Does the FCC router rule change this decision?

Not the router-versus-mesh question. It does affect which brands can launch new models this year: as of mid-September 2026, eero, Netgear and ASUS held FCC conditional approvals while TP-Link did not, and models already authorized can still be sold. We cover that in our large-house guides.

## What this means for you

Picture a 900 sq ft two-bedroom apartment with the router on a bookshelf near the front door. Move it to the middle of the apartment before doing anything else. A single router is almost certainly enough.

Now picture a 2,400 sq ft two-story house with the router downstairs and a bedroom upstairs that gets one bar. That's the classic mesh case, though a single node halfway up the stairs may be all you need.

And a 4,000 sq ft brick ranch with the router at one end is a mesh case even though it's one floor, because the walls and distance both work against you.

## Bottom line

Test first, try the free fixes, and buy mesh only if the far rooms still struggle. If you do, two units are a reasonable start, and an Ethernet cable between them is the best upgrade you can give the system.
