Your router is end-of-life if its maker has stopped selling it and stopped releasing software updates for it, including security fixes. You can check in about five minutes: read the model and hardware version off the label, then look that model up on the maker's support or end-of-life page. If the maker says it is unsupported, plan to replace it.

This is a research-based guide drawn from an FBI advisory, two manufacturers' published policies and a Federal Trade Commission (FTC) page. We have not tested any specific router's security. Source dates and the date we checked each page are in the fact-check table below.

## Why does end-of-life matter for a router?

A router runs software, called firmware, and flaws are found in firmware over time. A supported router gets patches. An end-of-life router does not, so any flaw found after support ends stays open.

The FBI put this in a public service announcement dated May 7, 2025 (alert number I-050725-PSA). It said cyber actors used variants of a botnet called TheMoon against end-of-life routers, and that it had identified a new variant on end-of-life routers with remote administration turned on. A botnet is a network of hijacked devices controlled by criminals. The FBI said TheMoon does not need a password to infect a router and was first found on compromised routers in 2014. It also said routers dated 2010 or earlier likely no longer receive manufacturer updates.

Two limits on that finding matter for readers:

- **It describes one group of attacks, not every router.** The warning concerned end-of-life routers, and the newest variant it named involved remote administration being enabled. It does not say every old router is compromised.
- **The advisory page names no router models.** Some news sites have published model lists attributed to the FBI. We could not find a list on the advisory page itself, so we do not reproduce one. Use your maker's own page instead.

## How to check if your router is end-of-life

1. **Find the model and hardware version.** Both are normally printed on a sticker on the bottom or back. TP-Link states that if no version is shown, the product is version 1 (v1). Hardware versions matter because a maker may support one version of a model and not another.
2. **Find your maker's support or end-of-life page.** Netgear publishes an End of Service page and policy. TP-Link points to separate end-of-life lists for Home Network and Smart Home products on its Omada support site; the page we checked is dated September 24, 2026 and says the list is "constantly being updated and subject to change without notice."
3. **Look for the model on the list or in the maker's support downloads.** If the model is listed as end of service, treat it as unsupported.
4. **Check the router's own admin page for its firmware version,** then compare it with the newest version on the maker's download page for your exact model and hardware version. A router that is behind the newest firmware is a separate problem you can fix by updating.
5. **If your ISP supplied it, ask the ISP.** The FTC advises checking with your ISP about whether it sends automatic updates to a router it provided.

If a maker does not publish an end-of-life list for your model, you cannot be certain of its status. In that case, a long gap since the last firmware release for your model is a warning sign, but it is our rule of thumb, not an official test.

## How long do makers support routers?

There is no industry-wide standard. Here is what we could verify from the two makers' own pages:

| Maker | What it publishes | What it says |
|---|---|---|
| Netgear | End of Service (EOS) policy | For one year after the last sale date it may provide software updates and feature enhancements. For three years after it may provide critical bug fixes and security patches, as Netgear determines. After three years, no software or firmware updates, including security updates. The last sale date can be limited to a particular product version in one country or region. |
| TP-Link | End-of-life product lists (Home Network, Smart Home) on its Omada support site | The lists are updated continuously, and identifying your product needs the model and hardware version from the label. We did not retrieve TP-Link's policy text on how long support lasts, so we make no claim about it. |

Netgear's example makes the timing concrete. If a router's last sale date were June 1, 2024 (a made-up date for illustration), Netgear's stated three-year window would end on June 1, 2027. Your own router's date would come from Netgear's list, not from this example. Netgear says the clock starts at the last sale date, not the date you bought the router, so a router bought late in its sales life has a shorter support period left than the box suggests.

Netgear's policy also notes that products sold through carriers or network operators may be supported by those providers instead.

## What should you do if your router is end-of-life?

**Replace it when you can.** The FBI recommends replacing end-of-life routers with a supported model where possible. When shopping, look for a maker that publishes a support policy for the model. Our [router buying guide for large homes]({{< relref "/guides/best-wifi-router-for-large-house" >}}) covers current picks, and [Wi-Fi 6E vs. Wi-Fi 7]({{< relref "/guides/wifi-6e-vs-wifi-7" >}}) explains which standard is worth paying for.

**Until then, reduce exposure.** These are the FBI's suggestions, plus one from the FTC:

- Turn off remote management in the router settings, save, and reboot. The FBI lists it among its steps for the variant it identified, and the FTC also says to turn it off.
- Install any firmware update still available for your model.
- Use a long, random, unique router password. The FBI suggests 16 to 64 characters and turning off password hints.
- If you suspect a problem, update the firmware, change your passwords and reboot, then consider a replacement.

These steps lower your risk. They do not fix flaws the maker will never patch, so treat them as a bridge to a replacement and not a long-term plan. Our [router hardening checklist]({{< relref "/guides/router-hardening-checklist" >}}) lists the other settings worth changing, and [WPS and UPnP]({{< relref "/guides/should-you-disable-wps-and-upnp" >}}) covers two more features to review.

**Check the security mode too.** The FTC says routers that support only WPA or WEP are "outdated and not secure," and advises considering a new router if yours cannot use WPA2 or WPA3 even after an update. Our [WPA2 vs. WPA3 guide]({{< relref "/guides/wpa2-vs-wpa3" >}}) explains the difference.

**Report an attack.** The FBI says victims can file a complaint at ic3.gov and should contact their account providers to secure accounts.

## Bottom line

Check your router's label and your maker's end-of-service page today. If it is supported, keep the firmware current. If it is unsupported, replace it, and in the meantime turn off remote management and reboot. Age alone is not the test; the maker's support status for your exact model and hardware version is.
