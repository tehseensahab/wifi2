The best mesh Wi-Fi for gigabit internet depends on how fast your plan really is. For a 1 Gbps plan, a system with gigabit Ethernet ports, such as TP-Link Deco X55 or Google Nest Wifi Pro, can carry the full plan into your main unit. If your plan is faster than 1 Gbps, or you expect to upgrade, buy 2.5 GbE ports: eero 7 is the lowest-priced multi-gig system whose price we could confirm, and eero says it is ideal for plans up to 2.5 Gbps. If you pay for a multi-gig plan and can't run Ethernet between rooms, a tri-band Wi-Fi 7 system such as eero Pro 7, Netgear Orbi 770 or TP-Link Deco BE63 adds a third (6 GHz) radio, which gives it more wireless capacity than the dual-band systems here.

This guide is research-based. We haven't tested these systems ourselves; specs come from each maker's US product pages, and prices were checked on October 24, 2026.

## Where gigabit speed gets lost in a mesh system

A mesh system has three places that can cap your speed. On a 1 Gbps plan, in our view, the internet port is the least of them.

### 1. The port your modem or ONT plugs into

A gigabit Ethernet port can't pass a full 1,000 Mbps of internet traffic, because some of each frame is protocol overhead. eero, for example, lists its eero 6+ at "up to 940 Mbps" wired. On a 1 Gbps plan that is close enough. On a 1.2 Gbps or 2 Gbps plan, a gigabit port throws the difference away before Wi-Fi is involved. That is the main case where multi-gig ports change what you get from your plan, and they can also help if your ISP provisions slightly above 1 Gbps; our guide to [multi-gig routers](/guides/do-you-need-a-multi-gig-router/) covers the port types in more detail.

### 2. The link between mesh units

Each satellite has to get your traffic back to the main unit. With wireless backhaul, that link shares radio capacity and weakens with distance and walls, so a satellite two rooms away will not deliver your full plan, whatever the box says. Wired backhaul, where satellites connect to the main unit by Ethernet, removes most of that loss. If you can run cable, see [wired vs. wireless backhaul](/guides/mesh-wifi-wired-vs-wireless-backhaul/). In our view, a cheaper system with wired backhaul will often outperform a pricier one relying on wireless backhaul.

### 3. Your devices

The numbers on mesh boxes, such as BE10000 or AXE5400, are the sum of each band's theoretical link rate. Most phones and laptops connect on one band at a time (Wi-Fi 7 devices with multi-link operation can combine bands), with fewer antennas than the router, and real throughput is lower than any link rate. In our analysis, a single wireless device often can't use a full 1 Gbps plan; the extra matters most when several people are busy at once.

## Which to buy, by plan and home

### 1 Gbps plan, no plans to upgrade: Deco X55 or Nest Wifi Pro

If your plan is 1 Gbps or slower, gigabit ports aren't a bottleneck worth paying to remove. **TP-Link Deco X55** is a dual-band Wi-Fi 6 system with three gigabit ports per unit, and TP-Link lists its 5 GHz radio at a 2,402 Mbps link rate using 160 MHz channels. It is the simplest fit for a 1 Gbps plan, especially if you can wire the units together.

**Google Nest Wifi Pro** suits the same plan if you want to manage Wi-Fi in the Google Home app or need a Thread border router for smart-home devices; Google lists one built in. Google's spec page says its two Ethernet ports "support 1 Gbps wired speeds per router," so it will not pass more than gigabit wired. Nest Wifi Pro was listed from $199.99 on the US Google Store on October 24, 2026. Check its update window before buying: our [Nest Wifi replacement guide](/guides/google-nest-wifi-replacement/) covers Google's guaranteed-update date.

### 1 Gbps now, faster later: eero 7

**eero 7** has two 2.5 GbE ports per unit and the lowest price we could confirm among the multi-gig systems here, and eero says it is "ideal for internet plans up to 2.5 Gbps." It is dual-band (2.4 and 5 GHz), so it has no 6 GHz radio; eero lists wireless speeds up to 1.8 Gbps. That makes it a good match for a gigabit plan today with headroom on the wired side if your ISP offers 2 Gbps later. The 2-pack was $284.99 at eero.com on October 24, 2026. Our [eero 7 vs. eero Pro 7 vs. eero Max 7 comparison](/guides/eero-7-vs-eero-pro-7-vs-eero-max-7/) goes deeper on the differences within eero's lineup.

### Multi-gig plan or a large home without Ethernet: eero Pro 7, Orbi 770 or Deco BE63

If you pay for 2 Gbps or more, or you rely on wireless backhaul across a big floor plan, a tri-band Wi-Fi 7 system gives you a 6 GHz radio on top of 2.4 and 5 GHz. The extra band adds capacity for client traffic and for the links between units, though how each system divides it isn't something these makers' spec pages state.

- **eero Pro 7** has two 5 GbE ports per unit, and eero calls it "ideal for internet plans up to 5 Gbps." It is the only system here with ports faster than 2.5 Gbps. $549.99 for a 2-pack at eero.com on October 24, 2026.
- **Netgear Orbi 770** has 2.5 Gig ports on both the router and satellites, and Netgear claims 5,500 sq ft for the 2-pack (RBE772). $549.99 for the 2-pack and $699.99 for the 3-pack at netgear.com on October 24, 2026.
- **TP-Link Deco BE63** has four 2.5 Gbps ports on every unit, including satellites (Orbi 770's satellites have two), which helps if you want to wire a TV, console and desktop at each satellite. TP-Link claims 5,800 sq ft for a 2-pack. We couldn't confirm a current price today.

With 2.5 Gbps ports, Orbi 770 and Deco BE63 will cap a wired connection at 2.5 Gbps; eero Pro 7's 5 GbE ports leave room for faster plans. On a 2 Gbps plan, all three are fine on the wired side.

## Before you buy

Find out what your ISP actually provisions and what your modem or fiber terminal (ONT) outputs. A gigabit-only port on the ISP's equipment caps you just as a gigabit port on the router does. If your ISP's gateway also does routing, put it in bridge mode or use the mesh system in access point mode to avoid double NAT; our [bridge mode guide](/guides/bridge-mode-double-nat-own-router/) explains how.

Then test the speed at the main unit with a wired computer before judging the Wi-Fi. If the wired test reaches your plan and a far room doesn't, the backhaul is the problem, not the internet connection.
