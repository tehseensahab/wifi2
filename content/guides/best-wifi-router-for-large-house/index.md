For most large U.S. homes, the best Wi-Fi router is really a mesh system: two or three matching units spread around the house instead of one powerful box in a corner. Independent testing site RTINGS ranks the TP-Link Deco 7 Pro BE63 first for large homes, but a 2026 FCC rule means TP-Link, unlike eero, Netgear and ASUS, did not have approval to sell new models as of mid-September reporting.

That regulatory wrinkle is new this year and most "best router" lists haven't caught up with it. Below: how to decide between a router and mesh, what to look for, which systems fit which houses, and where to put them.

*WiFi Weekly has not physically tested the products below. Performance statements come from the named independent reviewers; specifications come from manufacturers and are labeled as such.*

## Do I need a single router or a mesh system for a large house?

A single router is one transmitter. Wi-Fi signal drops with distance and with every floor, wall and appliance it passes through, so a router in the downstairs living room can be fast next to the couch and weak in an upstairs bedroom no matter how expensive it is.

Mesh systems fix that by placing several units around the house. Your phone connects to whichever is closest, and the units pass traffic to each other. Netgear's own guidance notes that concrete walls, large footprints and multiple floors all hurt performance, and it recommends mesh for those homes.

A single router can still be enough in a one-story, open layout where you can put it near the middle of the house. TP-Link, for example, rates a single Deco BE63 at 2,500 square feet.

Treat every "covers up to X square feet" figure as a manufacturer claim made under favorable conditions. Netgear's own fine print says real coverage is reduced by building construction and interference.

## What matters when choosing for a big house?

Five things separate a good large-home system from a poor one.

**Backhaul.** This is how the units talk to each other. Tri-band systems can dedicate a band to this job. Dual-band systems share their 5 GHz band between your devices and the link between units, which is the compromise in cheaper models. The best backhaul is an Ethernet cable between units, if your house has or can get one.

**Ports.** If you plan to wire units together or have a fast fiber or cable plan, 2.5 Gbps or faster ports keep the wiring from becoming the bottleneck.

**Software and accounts.** Some systems can only be managed through a phone app tied to a company account. That's fine for most people, but you should know it before you buy.

**Ongoing costs.** Some brands hold advanced features, like ad blocking and content filtering, behind an optional subscription.

**Update support.** A router you keep for five years needs security updates. This is now a regulatory question too, covered below.

Wi-Fi generation is on this list only at the bottom. If a Wi-Fi 6E system with better placement and wired backhaul costs less than a Wi-Fi 7 system, it may serve a large house better.

### The independent-testing favorite: TP-Link Deco 7 Pro BE63

RTINGS names the BE63 its best pick for large homes and praises the small, easy-to-place design. TechRadar also named it its top mesh pick this year. Tom's Guide found the system's throughput strong for the price but noted it had trouble keeping up at longer distances, and the reviewer measured a range of about 100 feet.

Two details from TP-Link matter. The system's headline speed of roughly 9.5 Gbps requires Wi-Fi 7 devices that support Multi-Link Operation and 6 GHz, which most phones and laptops sold today don't. And the system is managed only through TP-Link's Deco app with a TP-Link account, according to Tom's Hardware.

The model was renamed "Deco 7 Pro BE63" in early 2026, per Dong Knows Tech, so you may see both names.

**Skip it if** you object to TP-Link's app-and-account requirement, or if you're uneasy about buying from a brand whose U.S. regulatory status is unresolved. (Owning one remains legal and supported; see below.)

### The simplest setup with room to grow: eero Pro 7 and eero Max 7

Both are tri-band Wi-Fi 7 systems from Amazon. The Pro 7 has two 5 Gbps ports, which suits a 2 Gbps to 5 Gbps internet plan. The Max 7 adds two 10 Gbps ports and is built for very fast plans, lots of wired gear or homes where you'll cable several units together.

eero's basic system works without a subscription, but advanced features sit behind eero Plus, which was reported at $99.99 a year in 2025. Confirm the current price before you count on it.

**Skip it if** you don't want your network managed from an Amazon-run app, or if you don't need multi-gig ports and can buy less hardware.

### The budget option: Netgear Orbi 370 series

This is Netgear's most affordable Wi-Fi 7 Orbi. Dong Knows Tech points out that it is the first dual-band Wi-Fi 7 Orbi, with no 6 GHz band. Digital Reviews Network notes that it relies on shared 5 GHz backhaul, with the option of 2.5 GbE wired backhaul.

Reviewers describe it as a capable system for homes that have outgrown one router. Buyer reports on retail sites mention weaker satellites in dense, interference-heavy areas, though that is anecdotal.

**Skip it if** you live in a crowded apartment building and can't wire the units together, or if you own several Wi-Fi 7 devices and want 6 GHz.

### Other options worth checking

ASUS received a conditional approval covering its full router lineup in early September 2026, according to Tom's Hardware, and its ZenWiFi mesh line is included. RTINGS lists the ZenWiFi BT10 and ET9 among its reviewed models. We did not verify current specifications or pricing for those, so check ASUS's U.S. site and RTINGS before choosing one.

## How does the FCC router ban affect what I buy?

On March 23, 2026, the FCC added consumer-grade routers produced in foreign countries to its Covered List, which bars new models from getting FCC authorization. The FCC says this does not affect routers people already own, and does not stop retailers from selling models authorized before that date.

Companies can apply for an exemption called a Conditional Approval from the Department of War or Department of Homeland Security. Netgear and eero were approved in April 2026, and ASUS followed in September. TP-Link had not been approved as of the reporting we reviewed, and says it is a U.S.-headquartered company.

On firmware, the FCC's engineering bureau issued a waiver on May 8, 2026, moving the cutoff for software updates on covered routers from March 1, 2027 to January 1, 2029, according to CEPRO's report.

What this means in practice:

- If you own a TP-Link router, you don't have to replace it.
- Existing TP-Link models can still be sold, but new ones can't be introduced until TP-Link gets an approval. Stock of older models may thin out over time.
- Conditional approvals run up to 18 months, so they will need to be renewed.
- If security-update support over the next five years is your top concern, an approved brand carries less regulatory uncertainty. That's a risk judgment, not a finding that any brand's hardware is unsafe.

## Where should I put the units in a large house?

Placement often matters more than the model.

Picture a two-story house with the router downstairs at the front and a bedroom upstairs at the back that gets one bar. The instinct is to put the second unit in that bedroom. That usually backfires, because the unit needs a strong link to the main router to pass on. Put it roughly halfway, such as an upstairs hallway near the stairs, where it still gets a good signal from the first unit and can reach the bedroom.

A few rules of thumb follow from that:

- Put units in open areas, off the floor, not inside cabinets or behind a TV.
- Avoid placing a unit right next to a big metal object or a thick masonry wall.
- If you can run Ethernet between units, do it. A wired link removes the wireless hop and is the most reliable upgrade for big or older houses. If your house has coax outlets, MoCA adapters can carry Ethernet-class links over them, though that is outside what we compared here.
- Don't buy more units than you need. Extra nodes placed too close together don't help.

## What this means for you

If your house is one story and open, try a single router in the center first. If it has two or more floors, thick walls, or an odd shape, start with a two-unit mesh system and add a third only if a dead spot remains.

If you have fast fiber or cable of 2 Gbps or more, or plan to wire your house, prioritize 2.5 Gbps-plus ports and wired backhaul over the Wi-Fi generation. If you have a modest plan, a cheaper system with the units placed well will feel the same.

And if you already have a mesh system that reaches everywhere, replacing it for the Wi-Fi 7 label alone is unlikely to change your day-to-day experience.

## Bottom line

For a large house, buy for coverage first: a mesh system, positioned well and wired if possible. On independent testing, the TP-Link Deco 7 Pro BE63 leads RTINGS' large-home list, but eero, Netgear Orbi and ASUS are the brands with FCC conditional approvals for new models as of September 2026. Check the FCC's Conditional Approvals list before you buy, since it has been changing.
