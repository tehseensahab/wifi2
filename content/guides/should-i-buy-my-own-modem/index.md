**Buy your own modem only if you have cable internet and your bill shows a separate monthly fee for the provider's modem or gateway.** In that case a modem your provider certifies for your speed tier removes the fee, and federal law bars the provider from charging you for equipment you supply. If the provider's equipment is already included in your plan price, which Xfinity says of its "latest" plans (it doesn't say which plans those are), owning a modem saves nothing. You also take on setup and troubleshooting, and at Xfinity you can't get its newest X-Class speed tiers on a customer-owned modem.

This guide is about the modem only: the box that connects to the cable line. If you're deciding whether to replace the provider's Wi-Fi, see our guide to [buying your own router vs. using your provider's equipment](/guides/buy-your-own-router-vs-isp-equipment/). It is research-based, drawn from federal law, the FCC and Xfinity and Cox support pages checked on November 20, 2026. We have not tested any modem, and we don't quote rental fees, because they vary by provider, plan and area.

## Should I buy my own modem?

Yes if all three of these are true; otherwise keep the provider's equipment:

1. **You have cable internet.** A cable modem connects to the coax line. Fiber and 5G home internet normally connect through the provider's own terminal or gateway, so there's usually no modem for you to buy.
2. **You pay a monthly equipment fee.** The FCC's broadband label lists "Provider Monthly Fees," which it describes as fees "such as a modem rental," adding that "each fee must be listed out and described on the label." If no such fee appears on your label or bill, a modem of your own won't lower it.
3. **A retail modem is certified for your plan.** Xfinity says, "Your modem must work with Xfinity and support your internet speed." Cox says, "If it's not on the list, our service won't work."

| Your situation | Reasonable choice | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Cable plan, monthly modem or gateway rental fee on your bill | Consider buying a certified modem | The fee should go once you return the rental |
| Cable plan, equipment included in the plan price | Keep the provider's equipment | Owning one saves nothing on the bill |
| Xfinity X-Class plan | Keep Xfinity's equipment | Xfinity says customer-owned modems are "not currently compatible with X-Class speeds" |
| Xfinity upgraded area, want the faster upload tiers | Buy only a device Xfinity certifies for them | Xfinity says retail options are "limited" |
| You rent a voice modem for home phone | Check voice requirements first | Phone service needs a compatible eMTA |
| Fiber or 5G home internet | Nothing to buy | The provider's terminal or gateway connects to the line |

## Can my provider charge me for a modem I own?

No. Under 47 U.S.C. 562, a provider of fixed broadband internet access service "may not charge a consumer for— (1) using covered equipment provided by the consumer." The law defines covered equipment as "equipment (such as a router) employed on the premises of a person" to receive the service, which, in our reading, covers a cable modem as well.

The same section bars rental charges for equipment the provider never gave you, or for equipment "the consumer has returned to the provider," except for the period between when you received it and when you returned it. So the rental fee should stop once the provider has its equipment back. Cox states it plainly on its own page: "No, there is no charge to use your own modem."

## How long until my own modem pays for itself?

Divide the modem's price by the monthly rental fee on your bill:

**Months to break even = modem price ÷ monthly rental fee**

With hypothetical round numbers, a $150 modem that removes a $15 monthly fee pays for itself in 10 months ($150 ÷ $15 = 10), and everything after that is savings for as long as the modem stays certified for your plan. If the fee is small or your plan may move to a tier your modem can't support, the payback stretches out. Use your own bill's fee and the price you actually pay. The FCC glossary also lists "the purchase of a modem or other equipment" as a possible one-time fee, so a provider-sold modem shows up there rather than as a monthly charge.

## Which modem will work with my provider?

Check your provider's own list for your exact speed tier before you buy. Don't rely on a retailer's "works with" label alone.

- **Xfinity:** use the My Device Info tool on Xfinity's support site. Xfinity warns, "If your device isn't supported, it won't work with Xfinity." For gigabit service it adds, "Your device must support Gigabit speeds." Our [best modem for Xfinity guide](/guides/best-modem-for-xfinity/) covers models by plan.
- **Cox:** Cox says you can buy your own modem "as long as the equipment is certified to work on our network," and asks you to check its list "to make sure the modem you've chosen supports your internet speed tier." It adds that a used modem "connected to another account" can't be activated.
- **Other cable providers:** look for the provider's own approved or certified device list on its support site.

A modem's DOCSIS version is one of the specs that limits which plans it can handle; our [DOCSIS 3.0 vs. 3.1 vs. 4.0 guide](/guides/docsis-3-0-vs-3-1-vs-4-0-cable-modem/) explains the limits of each.

## What do I give up with my own modem?

**Provider support.** Cox says, "Only modems and devices purchased or rented directly from Cox are supported by Cox Customer Support." Xfinity says that with your own device you are responsible for "Keeping your device updated and secure," "Troubleshooting problems with your device" and "Setting up and managing your WiFi security."

**Free upgrades and perks.** Cox lists as a downside of owning: "None of the free perks included with Panoramic Wifi," including its "Upgrade Commitment—free equipment upgrades every three years." It also warns that "Installation, maintenance and upgrades may be expensive."

**Xfinity's newest speed tiers.** In areas Xfinity has upgraded, it says upload speeds are "up to 5–10 times faster than before, ranging from 75–200 Mbps," but "there are limited retail modems, eMTAs or gateways available in the marketplace" that support them. Xfinity says devices that aren't certified for the upgrade "should work for service tier speeds available before the recent upgrade." For X-Class plans, which Xfinity describes as offering "equally fast upload and download speeds above 200 Mbps" in limited areas, "customer-owned modems are not currently compatible."

**Home phone, unless you buy a voice modem.** Xfinity says, "To use Xfinity Voice, you'll need a compatible embedded Multimedia Terminal Adapter (eMTA)," which you can rent. It also says, "We do not guarantee the compatibility of Xfinity Voice with any other service, system or equipment, including customer-supplied eMTAs." If you rely on a home phone line through your cable company, check its voice requirements before returning a rented voice modem.

**Wi-Fi, if your rental is a gateway.** If your rental is a combined modem and router (a gateway), returning it also removes your Wi-Fi. A standalone modem has no Wi-Fi; Cox notes you "will need to purchase a separate router to pair with your modem" unless you buy a combined unit. Budget for that, and see our [best Wi-Fi router for a large house](/guides/best-wifi-router-for-large-house/) if coverage is part of the reason you're switching.

## How do I switch to my own modem?

1. **Find the fee.** Look for a modem, gateway or equipment line on your bill, and check the Provider Monthly Fees section of your plan's broadband label.
2. **Check the provider's list for your speed tier.** Use Xfinity's My Device Info, Cox's certified list or your provider's equivalent. At Xfinity, check whether your area has the upgraded upload tiers you want.
3. **Check what else the rental does.** Phone service needs a compatible voice modem; a gateway also provides your Wi-Fi.
4. **Buy the modem, plus a router if needed.** A new unit avoids Cox's warning about used modems still tied to another account.
5. **Install and activate it.** Cox says, "It's easy to install and activate your modem yourself." Follow your own provider's activation instructions.
6. **Return the rental and keep the receipt.** Federal law bars rental charges after you return the equipment, except for the time you had it.
7. **Check your next bill.** Confirm the equipment fee is gone. If it isn't, ask the provider to remove it.
