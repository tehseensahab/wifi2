Cable is the better choice if you upload a lot, or need steady speeds in the evening; if you host games or cameras that need port forwarding, confirm what any provider gives you first. 5G home internet is the better fit if your household mostly streams and browses and the signal at your address is strong, and T-Mobile lets you try it with a 15-day Test Drive. The biggest gap in the providers' own figures is upload: T-Mobile's typical 5G upload range tops out at 56 Mbps, while Comcast's published medians for its upgraded Xfinity cable tiers are about 114 to 209 Mbps up.

This guide compares the two using the speed, latency and network-management disclosures that T-Mobile, Verizon and Comcast (Xfinity) publish. It is research-based: we have not measured these services ourselves. If fiber is also offered at your address, start with our [fiber vs. cable vs. 5G home internet guide](/guides/fiber-vs-cable-vs-5g-home-internet/) instead.

## 5G home internet vs. cable: the quick verdict

| Your situation | Better fit | Why |
|---|---|---|
| You upload big files, back up to the cloud or live stream | Cable | Typical 5G upload ranges run about 10 to 56 Mbps on most plans (up to 75 Mbps on Verizon's top plan); upgraded cable tiers measure over 100 Mbps up |
| You need steady speed at 8 p.m. | Cable | T-Mobile gives Home Internet heavy-data-user priority; Verizon may prioritize data behind other traffic on certain plans |
| You host game servers, run a camera system or need port forwarding | Cable (check first) | Neither 5G carrier's disclosure says whether you get a public IP address |
| You mostly stream, browse and make video calls | Either; 5G is worth a trial | Typical 5G download ranges on most plans run into the hundreds of Mbps |
| You want to test before committing | 5G | T-Mobile's 15-day Test Drive refunds service charges if you cancel |
| The 5G signal at your address is weak | Cable | 5G speed depends on your address and the cell site you connect to |

## How do 5G home internet and cable speeds compare?

These are the providers' own figures. T-Mobile and Verizon publish typical ranges, roughly the 25th to 75th percentile of network tests, so roughly a quarter of tests come in below the low number. Comcast publishes median results for Xfinity tiers. The two kinds of figures are not measured the same way, so treat the comparison as a guide, not a head-to-head test.

| Plan | Download | Upload | Latency |
|---|---|---|---|
| T-Mobile 5G Home Internet, All-In and Amplified plans | 133–415 Mbps | 12–55 Mbps | 16–28 ms |
| T-Mobile 5G Home Internet, Rely plans | 87–318 Mbps | 14–56 Mbps | 18–36 ms |
| T-Mobile 5G Home Internet, Lite plans | 52–241 Mbps | 11–30 Mbps | 18–36 ms |
| Verizon 5G Home (up to 100 Mbps, mid-band) | 25–85 Mbps | 5–10 Mbps | 50–70 ms |
| Verizon 5G Home (up to 300 Mbps) | 85–300 Mbps | 10–20 Mbps | 35–55 ms |
| Verizon 5G Home Plus (up to 500 Mbps) | 200–500 Mbps | 20–35 Mbps | 35–55 ms |
| Verizon 5G Home Ultimate (up to 1 Gig) | 300–1,000 Mbps | 25–75 Mbps | 35–55 ms |
| Xfinity cable, 300 Mbps mid-split tier (median at peak) | 351 Mbps | 114 Mbps | 16 ms |
| Xfinity cable, 1,000 Mbps mid-split tier (median at peak) | 1,142 Mbps | 114 Mbps | 16 ms |
| Xfinity cable, 2,000 Mbps mid-split tier (median at peak) | 2,088 Mbps | 206 Mbps | 14 ms |

How to read the table:

- **T-Mobile** says its ranges are "Based on internal analysis and projections from third-party, crowd-sourced data" and are "projections based on roughly the 25th and 75th percentiles of network tests." The page shows no date.
- **Verizon's** page was last updated October 29, 2025. Its 5G Home plan names and tiers may have changed since; the figures are what Verizon still publishes.
- **Xfinity's** figures come from a Comcast summary published December 18, 2024, covering tiers on sale as of April 9, 2024, with peak defined as weekday evenings from 7 p.m. to 11 p.m. Comcast's footnote says its figures are "Based on tests to the cable modem gateway." That is a shorter path than a test to an internet server, so its speed and latency numbers will likely look better than what a speed test app shows at home.
- **Not every cable home gets these upload speeds.** The rows above are Xfinity's upgraded "mid-split" tiers. Comcast's own table shows its Internet Essentials tier (75 Mbps, a low-income plan that is not listed as mid-split) at 11 Mbps up. Other cable companies publish their own figures.

**What this means for you:** on download, a good 5G signal can match a mid-range cable plan. On upload, cable in an upgraded area measures roughly 1.5 to 4 times the top of the typical 5G ranges. If you are unsure how much upload you need, our guide to [what a good upload speed is](/guides/good-upload-speed/) breaks it down by activity.

## Does 5G home internet slow down when the network is busy?

Yes, it can, and both carriers say so in their own policies. This is the main structural difference from cable.

- **T-Mobile:** "In general, T-Mobile Internet customers receive the same network prioritization as Mobile Wireless Heavy Data Users." This suggests phone customers on standard plans get priority when a site is congested. T-Mobile adds that for stationary plans "only available in limited areas (for example, Home Internet and Home Internet Plus), these customers should be less likely to notice congestion in general." Customers using "more than 1.2TB of data in a billing cycle" are "prioritized last on the network."
- **Verizon:** "On certain plans, we may prioritize your 5G and 4G LTE data behind other traffic." Verizon does not name the plans on that page. It also says that if "the cell site you are connected to begins experiencing high demand," speeds may be slower until demand drops.
- **Xfinity cable:** Comcast's disclosure says it "currently does not maintain a separate system to assist with managing times of congestion." Comcast's published medians for its upgraded tiers show peak-hour results within about 6 percent of off-peak.

**What this means for you:** if your internet use is heaviest at the same time as everyone else's (evenings and weekends), test 5G during those hours before you cancel cable.

## What else is different?

### Port forwarding and public IP addresses

Neither T-Mobile's nor Verizon's network-management page mentions public IP addresses, port forwarding or CGNAT, so we can't tell you what each plan provides. If you host a game server, run a security camera system that needs inbound access, or use remote desktop into your home, ask the carrier before switching, and read our explainer on [what CGNAT is and how to check for it](/guides/what-is-cgnat/). Comcast's disclosure says it "blocks a limited number of ports that are commonly used to send spam."

### Data limits

- T-Mobile lists no hard cap but deprioritizes Home Internet use above 1.2 TB per billing cycle, as of May 8, 2024.
- Verizon's Home Internet Lite plan says: "After 150 GB of data, speeds lowered up to 10 Mbps download and 3 Mbps upload." Its other 5G Home plans list no such limit on that page.
- Xfinity says some of its plans are usage-based: "usage that exceeds a monthly usage threshold is subject to an additional charge," with an unlimited-data option for a monthly fee. Check your own plan in the Xfinity account.

### Video quality

Verizon says it streams video "at 1080p to devices with larger screens and on the 5G Home, 5G Home Plus" and several other plans, "and at 4K on the 5G Home Ultimate plans." T-Mobile's network-management page does not state a video resolution limit for Home Internet.

### Equipment and your own router

5G home internet uses the carrier's gateway. If its Wi-Fi doesn't reach your whole home, our [best router for 5G home internet guide](/guides/best-router-for-5g-home-internet/) covers adding your own router or mesh system.

## Who should not switch from cable to 5G?

- Households that upload a lot: creators, people backing up photo libraries, anyone sending large work files.
- Gamers or home-lab users who need open ports or a public IP address, until the carrier confirms what you get.
- Homes where the 5G signal is weak indoors or the gateway can only sit in one poor spot.
- Anyone whose heaviest use falls in the evening peak and who can't tolerate slowdowns.

## How to try 5G home internet without cancelling cable

1. **Check availability at your exact address** on the carrier's site. Verizon says its plans vary by address and location, and both carriers' figures depend on the cell site you connect to.
2. **Use a trial.** T-Mobile's 15-day Test Drive gives "A one-time bill credit" for service charges if you cancel within 15 days. You must return the gateway within 45 days of cancelling or face a "non-return fee of up to $370." The trial is "Limited to one per account."
3. **Keep cable active** while you test.
4. **Test at your busiest hours.** Run speed tests in the evening, on video calls and while uploading, from the rooms you use most.
5. **Check port forwarding** if you need it, before your trial ends.
6. **Decide before day 15**, then return equipment to whichever provider you leave.
